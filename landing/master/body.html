Overview

Growth & usage

The question this answers is question 9: are we acquiring more people without building a larger returning userbase? Returning is the line to watch — first runs rise and fall with the store, but only returning activity means the product is holding anyone. Recorded events appear as flags along the top; hover one for what it was.

Retention curve

Share of each cohort still opening the app N days after installing, pooled over every cohort old enough to have reached that day. The dashed rule is the product's boundary: the trial ends after D14, so D15 is the first day on the free tier. A cohort too young for a day is left out of it entirely rather than counted as churned.

Crossing the boundary

The lifecycle as a funnel, and the transition that straddles the product decision. Both sides of it are measured over the same installs — only cohorts old enough for the later day — so a change cannot be an artefact of a shifting denominator.

Cohort retention

Rows are install cohorts, columns are milestones either side of each boundary. Colour is retention strength. A hatched cell means that cohort has not lived that long yet — which is not the same as 0%, and is never averaged in. Cohorts under 10 installs are flagged, because a percentage over six people is mostly noise. Click a row to inspect it below.

Who is using it today

Today's active installs by how old they are. If this is dominated by the youngest bars, activity is being propped up by acquisition; a long tail means older installs are still showing up. Installs older than the counter are charted here too — their age came in with the ping, only their cohort size is unknown.

Purchase timing

How old an install was when it subscribed. D15 has its own column deliberately: if the end of the trial is what converts people, this is where it shows up.

Activation

How old an install was when it saved its first HRV reading — the moment the app starts working at all. Same buckets as purchase timing, so the two are read off one axis. A healthy onboarding puts nearly everything in D0; weight further right means people are leaving the wizard without a reading and coming back for it later. Activation fires once per install, so unlike the active counts these rows really do count people.

How the first reading is taken

Which sensor the activating reading used. Apple Watch is offered on iPhone only, so its share is a share of iOS and not of everyone — the platform filter above is how to read it honestly. A camera-heavy split says people are activating on the option that needs no hardware, which activates more of them but produces the weakest signal.

Opened vs measured

Every day, how many installs opened the app and how many saved an HRV reading. The gap between the two lines is people launching the app without using it, which is the shape that precedes churn and which retention alone cannot see. Both counters fire at most once per install per US Eastern day, so the share underneath is a share of people. Days before the reading counter shipped are a gap in the line, not a zero.

The habit curve

Retention and measuring on one axis, by install age. Opened is the ordinary retention curve; measured is the share of the same cohorts that took a reading on that day. The distance between them is the part of the userbase that is still showing up and no longer measuring — the thing to watch after the trial ends at D14. A cohort too young for a day is left out of it, and so is one whose day N fell before the reading counter existed: neither is a zero.

iOS vs Android, day by day

Active installs each day, split by the store the build runs on. This chart is always unfiltered — it is what the platform filter above is a slice of, so filtering it would leave nothing to compare. Pings from builds that shipped before the platform marker existed carry no store: they are real installs whose store we failed to record, so they are drawn as their own band rather than folded into either one. With a platform filter in force they are counted into both views, because putting them in neither is what made a filtered dashboard read as empty.

Weekday pattern

Whether the midweek acquisition peak is real, and whether it is worth anything. Each weekday carries six bars as averages per weekday, so a day with five Mondays in range is not flattered by having more of them: store downloads, first runs, returning activity, readings (which weekday people actually measure on, the number the morning reminder's time should follow), and then the two that matter most — purchases from your own sales ledger and subscribe pings from the app itself. The last two count the same event at different moments (the app only notices a subscription on its next launch), so they should track rather than match, and a persistent gap between them is worth chasing. The table below asks the harder question: do installs born midweek actually retain better, or are they just more numerous?

Monthly recurring revenue

The rate the book runs at on each day, stacked by plan — an annual plan counts as a twelfth of its price. A subscription is assumed to still run until you mark it cancelled under Edit data; nothing here is derived from the stores, because they tell this dashboard nothing about churn.

Bookings vs recognised revenue

Bookings is the cash that arrived that month. Recognised revenue spreads each purchase over the months it buys — an annual plan is one twelfth of its price in each of the twelve months after the sale. The gap between them is the annual book, and reading either one alone is how a month with one annual sale gets called a record or a collapse depending which number was on screen.

Every ping received

One row per (route, arrival day, cohort key) — which is exactly how the counter stores it, so nothing here is derived. Arrived is the day the server stamped; cohort is the day that install first ran, carried in the request path. Age is the gap between them, so an open ping at age 0 is a first run and a subscribe at age 20 is someone who paid three weeks in. The counter carries no device id and no body, which is why a row is a count and never a list of people. The range and platform filters above apply; unknown platform means a build that shipped before the store marker existed.

Store data

Downloads, impressions and sales, as the stores report them

Add / update a single day

Entries are keyed by date + platform, so re-entering a day overwrites it. Store reporting lags, so the date defaults to the latest day that can have data — never the current day. Sales are not here any more: a subscription's plan and the buyer's install date are properties of the purchase, not of the day, so they live in the Sales ledger below.

Bulk add / edit days

Build a grid for a stretch of days and fill it in one pass. Days you have already entered come back pre-filled, so this doubles as bulk editing. Paste straight from a spreadsheet — click the top-left cell you want to land on and paste; tab- or comma-separated blocks spill across and down. Enter / ↑ ↓ move down and up a column, Tab moves across. Rows that already hold data are marked • and can be deleted outright with the button at the end of the row.

Sales

Every purchase, its plan, and who bought it

Add a purchase

One row per purchase. The plan is what turns a price into recurring revenue — an annual plan at the same price is a twelfth of the MRR of a monthly one — and the install date is what lets the Sales view say how long people take to decide. Leave the install date blank if you cannot match the buyer; the purchase still counts everywhere except the days-to-purchase chart, which says what share of sales it is drawn from. Mark a subscription cancelled and it leaves MRR from that date; mark it refunded and it leaves every money figure entirely.

Paste a batch of purchases

One purchase per line, straight from a spreadsheet or a store export. Columns, tab- or comma-separated: date, platform, plan, price, install date, cancelled, note — everything after the price is optional. Platform reads anything containing “and”, “goog” or “play” as Android and everything else as iOS; plan reads “annual”/“year”/“yr” as annual, “life” as lifetime, and anything else as monthly. Paste a header row and the columns are read by name instead of by position, which is how the ledger's own Export CSV — it carries qty and refunded as well — comes back in unchanged. Nothing is written until you press Add.

All purchases

The whole ledger, newest first — all time, not the dashboard's selected range. This is the list you manage sales from; the Sales tab is where you read them against a period.

Spending

The ad spots you bought, and everything else the app costs to run

Ad spots

One line per thing you bought: what it was, the platform it ran on, what it cost, and the impressions and clicks it reported back. The cost lands whole on the start date — nothing is spread across the days it runs, because that is not how the money was spent. Leave the end date blank while a spot is still running and fill it in when it stops; both ends appear as flags on the Timeline, so a spike can be read against what was live. Every spot ever bought is listed, including ones that start in the future.

Other costs

Everything that is not an ad spot, newest first — developer programmes, hosting, tools, creative work, contract work, hardware. A recurring cost is entered once and charged on every occurrence, so a monthly bill needs one row rather than twelve. Advertising is not offered here: it is an ad spot, entered above, and entering it twice in two shapes would double it.

What happened

Store changes, press, anything else that might move the numbers

Events

Recorded once here and drawn on every calendar chart in the dashboard, so a spike six months from now still says what you did that week. Releases and ad spots flag themselves and are not entered here. Read them on the Timeline, where clicking a flag compares the fortnight either side of it.

Settings

How the app behaves, and what the stores keep

Trial length

This describes how your app behaves, so it lives here rather than in the filter bar — changing it re-derives every cohort metric. The limit is inclusive: with 14, a user is still in the trial on day 14 and leaves it on day 15.

Store commission

Sales amounts are entered at the customer-facing price, which is not what reaches the bank. This is the cut Apple and Google keep — 15% on the small-business programmes, 30% once you leave them. It is used everywhere profit is: the Costs view nets revenue down by it before subtracting spend, and always shows the gross figure beside the net one.

Notifications

A system notification for the same arrivals the corner cards announce — a download, a sale. These fire only while the dashboard is open and you are looking at something else (another window, another tab, another app), which is the case a toast in the corner is worth nothing in. On an iPhone or iPad this works only once the dashboard has been added to the home screen, from Safari's share menu.

Background alerts

The same two arrivals — a sale, a new install — sent to this device with the dashboard closed. The counter is checked once an hour on the server and one notification is sent per hour that brought news, so six installs in an afternoon is one banner, not six. Nothing here reads the store CSV or the sales ledger: both are typed in by hand, and an alert about your own typing is not an alert.

iPhone and iPad need iOS 16.4 or later and the dashboard added to the home screen — a Safari tab cannot receive these, and the button below will say so.

Backup, import & reset

CSV columns: date,platform,downloads,impressions,pageViews,updates,notes. Importing merges by date + platform, overwriting matches. A conversion-rate column is ignored — it is always derived from downloads ÷ impressions. sales and revenue columns are still read, and land in the Sales ledger as unclassified purchases rather than on the day. The CSV out is store days only — it carries no purchases, because a purchase is no longer a property of a day. Export the ledger from Edit data → Sales, or take the JSON backup, which carries everything.
