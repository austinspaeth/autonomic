Vitamin B1, thiamine, sits in an odd place. In one direction the science is a hundred years old and not remotely controversial: a real thiamine deficiency causes a fast heart rate, orthostatic intolerance and a sluggish gut, which is almost a description of a bad POTS day. In the other direction, the popular claim that high-dose thiamine fixes fatigue in people who are not actually deficient rests on much thinner ground. Both of those things are true at once, and most of the arguing online happens because people pick one and ignore the other.

These are educational field notes, not medical advice, and nothing here diagnoses or treats a condition. The aim is to give you the honest shape of the evidence so you and your clinician can make a sensible decision.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <strong>The short version.</strong> Thiamine runs the enzymes that turn food into cellular energy, and nerves are energy-hungry, so genuine B1 deficiency has long been known to cause tachycardia and orthostatic intolerance. The idea that extra thiamine helps people who are not deficient is plausible but unproven, backed mostly by small open-label studies and patient reports. It is very safe, it needs magnesium to work, and the smart approach is to check your real risk factors, discuss testing, and measure your own response if you trial it.

## Why thiamine matters for the autonomic nervous system

Thiamine is not a direct nerve tonic. Its job is more fundamental than that. Inside your cells, thiamine is converted to thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), the active form, and TPP is an essential cofactor for a handful of enzymes that sit at the center of energy production:

- **Pyruvate dehydrogenase**, which feeds the product of glucose breakdown into the citric acid cycle.
- **Alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase**, a step inside that cycle.
- **Transketolase**, in the pentose phosphate pathway.

Without enough TPP, those reactions slow down, aerobic ATP production falls, and cells lean harder on less efficient pathways. The tissues that notice first are the ones with the highest and least flexible energy demands: the heart, and the nervous system, including the autonomic nerves that regulate heart rate, blood vessels and digestion.

There is a second detail worth holding onto, because it explains a lot of frustration: thiamine stores are small. The body holds only about 25 to 30 mg, and that reserve runs down in roughly two to three weeks without adequate intake. So thiamine status is not a slow-moving thing like iron. It responds quickly to changes in demand, losses and intake, which is exactly why illness, poor appetite and medication can tip it.

## The thiamine and dysautonomia connection

This is the part that is genuinely well established. The deficiency disease, beriberi, has been described for well over a century, and its cardiovascular form reads like an autonomic case study.

"Wet" beriberi is the cardiovascular kind: a fast resting heart rate, a bounding high-output circulation, and fluid retention. "Dry" beriberi is the neurological kind, with peripheral neuropathy that can include the autonomic nerves, producing orthostatic hypotension and gastrointestinal dysmotility. The alcohol-associated form, Wernicke encephalopathy, is the one most doctors are trained to spot urgently. Across these forms, tachycardia, orthostatic intolerance and a slow gut are recurring features, and they improve when thiamine is replaced.

None of that is a claim about POTS specifically. It is the reason the idea is biologically reasonable at all: if a severe shortage of thiamine can drive those exact symptoms, then it is fair to ask whether a milder or functional shortage might contribute in some people. That is the question the modern interest is really built on.

## Could you be low without classic beriberi?

Most people reading this will not have frank beriberi. The more useful question is whether thiamine demand or losses are running higher than intake, which is sometimes called functional or subclinical deficiency. Several ordinary situations push in that direction, and people managing POTS, dysautonomia and long COVID tend to collect more than one of them.

A few of those deserve a sentence. Refined carbohydrate raises thiamine demand because metabolizing glucose is precisely what thiamine-dependent enzymes do, so a diet of mostly processed starch and sugar burns through B1 faster. Loop diuretics increase urinary thiamine loss, which matters because some people with POTS or heart issues take them. Gut problems reduce how much you absorb, and the overlap between dysautonomia and conditions like SIBO is well documented. And long COVID often brings a stretch of poor appetite and reduced intake at exactly the time metabolic demand is disturbed. If you want to go deeper on the gut angle, see our piece on [SIBO, histamine and the gut in dysautonomia](/insights/food/sibo-histamine-gut-dysautonomia/).

## What the evidence actually shows for POTS and long COVID

Here is the honest state of the field, stated plainly. There is no randomized controlled trial of thiamine supplementation in POTS or in long COVID. That is the single most important sentence in this article.

What exists instead is a mix of three things. First, solid and settled evidence that correcting a real deficiency relieves the autonomic features of beriberi. Second, a body of small, mostly open-label studies and case series, several associated with the late Dr. Antonio Costantini, reporting that high-dose thiamine improved fatigue in conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease, Hashimoto thyroiditis, fibromyalgia and post-infectious fatigue. These were largely uncontrolled, so they cannot separate a true effect from placebo or natural recovery. Third, a large and sincere volume of patient reports in long COVID, ME/CFS and dysautonomia communities, where thiamine (often benfotiamine or TTFD) is a popular trial.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <strong>Plausible is not proven.</strong> A convincing mechanism plus a pile of anecdotes is a reason to run a careful, measured trial with your doctor, not evidence that thiamine treats POTS, dysautonomia or long COVID. If you see it described as a cure, that is going well beyond what the data supports.
</div>

The sensible reading is this: thiamine is most likely to help the people who were genuinely short of it to begin with, and least likely to do much for people whose levels are already fine. That is true of most nutrients. It is also why the risk-factor check above matters more than the enthusiasm online. Fatigue in post-viral illness has many drivers, and thiamine is one candidate among several, which is the same lens we take in [why you are so tired with POTS or long COVID](/insights/postviral/why-am-i-so-tired-pots-long-covid-fatigue/).

## How do you test thiamine status?

Testing is possible but imperfect, which is part of why clinicians sometimes trial replacement rather than chase a number. The options you might discuss:

| Test | What it measures | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whole-blood or erythrocyte thiamine (TPP) | The active cofactor inside red cells | Generally preferred over plasma, which reflects recent intake more than stores |
| Erythrocyte transketolase activity | How well a thiamine-dependent enzyme works, with and without added TPP | A large jump when TPP is added suggests functional deficiency; the classic functional test |
| Serum thiamine | Circulating thiamine | Cheaper and common, but a poorer reflection of tissue stores |

No test is perfect, levels can shift quickly, and reference ranges vary between labs. Interpret results with your clinician rather than against a chart you found online, and remember that a borderline number in someone with clear risk factors is read differently from the same number in someone with none.

## Forms and doses, compared

If you and your clinician decide a trial is reasonable, the form matters. They are not interchangeable.

| Form | Character | Typically used for | Worth knowing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Thiamine HCl / mononitrate | Standard water-soluble | General supplementation, correcting deficiency | Cheap, well studied, the default |
| Benfotiamine | Fat-soluble derivative | Diabetic nerve problems; reaches higher blood levels | Most human data of the enhanced forms |
| TTFD (allithiamine) | Lipid-soluble, crosses membranes and the brain | Fatigue-focused protocols | Can cause a sulfur smell and GI upset; less studied |
| Sulbutiamine | Lipid-soluble, crosses the brain barrier | Fatigue and cognition | Limited long-term data |

Everyday supplements supply anywhere from 1 to 100 mg. The high-dose protocols people discuss for fatigue often use several hundred milligrams, sometimes up to 1 to 2 g per day, which is well outside ordinary supplementation and is squarely a place for clinician involvement. More absorbable is not automatically better: a form that reaches higher levels is also a larger effective dose, so it is not a free upgrade.

## Safety, magnesium and the interactions worth knowing

The reassuring part first. Thiamine is water-soluble, excess is excreted in the urine, and oral thiamine has very low toxicity with no established upper intake limit. Compared with fat-soluble vitamins, the margin for error is wide.

Two caveats are worth carrying anyway. First, **thiamine needs magnesium to become active**: the enzyme that converts thiamine to its working form is magnesium-dependent, so a magnesium deficiency can leave thiamine underperforming no matter how much you take. If you are low in magnesium, that is worth addressing in parallel, and we cover it in [magnesium, HRV and POTS](/insights/food/magnesium-hrv-pots/). Second, some people in the high-dose community describe a rocky first week on TTFD in particular, sometimes called a "paradoxical" reaction. It is not well characterized in trials, but it is one more reason to start modestly and under supervision rather than jumping to a gram a day.

The intravenous route is different and belongs entirely to clinicians, both because it is how severe deficiency is treated and because IV thiamine can, rarely, trigger a serious allergic reaction. Nothing in this article is about self-administering anything by that route.

## How to run a careful trial you can actually read

If you are going to try thiamine, the single most valuable thing you can do is make it measurable. A supplement trial with no baseline is just a story you tell yourself afterward, and recovery conditions are full of good weeks and bad weeks that have nothing to do with what you changed.

A workable protocol:

1. **Set a baseline.** For one to two weeks before you start, take a reading at the same time each morning, in the same position, while still. Morning HRV and resting heart rate are the core two. If orthostatic symptoms are your main issue, add a simple [stand test](/insights/hrv/hrv-baseline-how-to-build-and-use-it/). Jot a one-line symptom and energy note.
2. **Change one thing.** Start the thiamine, keep sleep, salt, fluids and other supplements as steady as you reasonably can, and keep measuring the same way.
3. **Read the trend, not the day.** Give it several weeks. Look for a sustained move in your own numbers and symptoms, not a single standout morning.
4. **Call it.** If your trend clearly improves and holds, that is worth discussing as a keeper. If nothing has budged after a fair trial, that is a real answer too, and it saves you money and another bottle in the cupboard.

## FAQ

**Does thiamine actually help POTS or dysautonomia?**
There is no randomized controlled trial of thiamine in POTS or dysautonomia, so it is not proven. What is well established is the reverse: a genuine thiamine deficiency can cause tachycardia, orthostatic intolerance and gut dysmotility, because thiamine is essential for the energy enzymes autonomic nerves rely on. The modern claim about high doses in people who are not deficient comes from small, mostly open-label studies and patient reports. That is a reason to explore it carefully with your clinician, not a demonstrated treatment.

**Who is actually at risk of being low in thiamine?**
Body stores are small and run down in two to three weeks without intake, so demand and losses matter. Higher risk includes regular alcohol use, a diet heavy in refined carbohydrates, loop diuretics such as furosemide, chronic gut problems or malabsorption, bariatric surgery, long stretches of poor appetite or vomiting, and possibly long-term metformin. People managing POTS or long COVID often tick several at once.

**What is the difference between thiamine HCl, benfotiamine and TTFD?**
Thiamine hydrochloride and mononitrate are the standard water-soluble forms, cheap and well studied. Benfotiamine is fat-soluble, reaches higher blood levels and is most studied for diabetic nerve problems. TTFD (allithiamine) and sulbutiamine are lipid-soluble forms that cross membranes more readily, which is why fatigue protocols favor them, though they are less studied and TTFD can cause a sulfur smell and GI upset. More absorbable is not automatically better or safer.

**How much thiamine is safe to take?**
Thiamine is water-soluble with very low oral toxicity and no established upper limit, because excess is excreted. Everyday supplements supply 1 to 100 mg. The high-dose protocols discussed for fatigue often use several hundred milligrams up to 1 to 2 g per day, which is well outside ordinary supplementation and is where a clinician should be involved. Thiamine also needs magnesium to work, so low magnesium can blunt the effect.

**How will I know if thiamine is doing anything?**
Treat it as an experiment. Take a week or two of baseline readings at the same time each morning (HRV, resting heart rate, a stand test if orthostatic symptoms dominate) alongside a quick symptom note, then start and keep measuring. A real effect is a sustained shift in your own trend over several weeks, not one good day. If nothing changes after a fair trial, that is useful information too.
