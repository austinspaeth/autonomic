If you have long COVID, "how long will this last?" is probably the question you most want answered and the one doctors are least able to answer. There is no single timeline. **For many people, long COVID eases within the first year, but a meaningful minority are still unwell two years after infection, and improvement tends to slow after the first year.** The useful detail is in the numbers, so here is what the follow-up studies show, who tends to recover faster and how to read your own trend.

## How long does long COVID usually last?

The clearest long-term picture comes from a Swiss study that followed 1,106 adults in Zurich for two years after a confirmed infection ([Ballouz et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2022-074425)). Everyone was infected with the original strain before they were vaccinated, and the researchers asked the same questions at fixed points afterwards.

Two things stand out. First, the share of people who were not back to normal fell from 22.9% at 6 months to 18.5% at a year, then only to 17.2% at two years. Second, the averages hide movement in both directions: between 6 and 24 months, 13.5% of people improved while 5.2% got worse.

"Not fully recovered" was a broad measure in this study, and it included people with mild leftover symptoms as well as people who were badly limited. The numbers also come from an unvaccinated group infected early in the pandemic, so they likely run higher than what someone infected today would face. But they show the shape that most studies share: early improvement, then a long, slower tail.

## What do the newest studies show?

**Most people improve, but the long COVID group itself is harder to shift.** The US RECOVER study followed 3,659 adults, almost all infected in the Omicron era, with surveys every 3 months for 15 months ([Thaweethai et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-65239-4)). About 10% met the study's long COVID threshold at 3 months. Of those people, 46% kept a persistently high symptom burden, 35% had symptoms that came and went at a high level and 19% improved, with most of that improvement in the first few months. A further 14% of the whole cohort did not meet the threshold at 3 months but had rising symptoms by 15 months, which the authors could not fully explain.

**National surveys suggest many people do get past it.** In the 2023 US National Health Interview Survey, 8.4% of adults said they had ever had long COVID and 3.6% said they had it at the time of the interview ([Vahratian et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.51151)). That gap suggests more than half of the people who ever had long COVID no longer did, though some of them had only recently caught COVID, so it is a rough guide rather than a recovery rate.

**Clinic patients recover more slowly.** A London specialist service followed 3,590 people referred with long COVID, many with severe symptoms ([Prashar et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2025-103884)). About a third (33.4%) reached the study's recovery mark during follow-up, and those who recovered took a median of about 202 days from the start of their symptoms. Fatigue barely changed on average across the group.

These numbers don't contradict each other as much as they look. Counting everyone who was infected gives an encouraging picture. Counting only people who already have long COVID that is severe enough to need a clinic gives a harder one. Where you fall depends partly on which group you are in.

## Who recovers from long COVID faster?

The London clinic study looked at what predicted recovery ([Prashar et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2025-103884)). In its models:

- **Vaccination before infection** was linked to roughly three times the rate of recovery, and vaccination after infection to a smaller increase.
- **Severe fatigue** was the strongest sign of slower recovery, and people with the highest fatigue scores recovered least.
- **Postural symptoms and dysautonomia**, the POTS-type symptoms of racing heart, dizziness and fatigue when upright, were linked to roughly half the recovery rate.
- **Muscle pain** was also linked to slower recovery.
- In the group who were never hospitalised, **men** recovered faster than women.

That is hard reading if you have POTS or severe fatigue, and it is better to know it than to be surprised by it. But these are averages from one clinic seeing more severe cases, not a forecast for any one person. Many people with dysautonomia after COVID do improve, often slowly. [Does POTS Get Better Over Time?](/insights/pots/does-pots-get-better-over-time/) covers what is known about that path specifically.

## Why do some people plateau?

Nobody can yet say why one person recovers in three months and another is still ill at three years. Researchers are studying several possible drivers, including lingering virus or viral fragments, immune changes, small blood clots and damage to the nerves that run the autonomic nervous system. [What Causes Long COVID?](/insights/postviral/what-causes-long-covid/) explains each of them in plain language.

One factor you can act on is post-exertional malaise (PEM), the delayed crash after doing too much. In a Dutch study, people with long COVID who exercised to exhaustion all developed PEM, and their muscle biopsies a day later showed worse mitochondrial function and some muscle fibre damage that healthy controls did not show ([Appelman et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-44432-3)). If every good stretch ends in a crash, the boom and bust cycle can keep you stuck. NICE's guidance for ME/CFS, a condition some people with long COVID and PEM go on to meet the criteria for, is not to push through symptoms and to raise activity only from a stable base ([NICE, 2021](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206/chapter/Recommendations)). [Why Do I Feel Worse the Day After a Good Day?](/insights/mecfs/why-do-i-feel-worse-the-day-after-a-good-day/) explains how the delayed crash works.

If your long COVID has lasted more than six months and includes PEM, it is worth asking whether you meet the criteria for ME/CFS, because that changes what advice applies. [Is Long COVID the Same as ME/CFS?](/insights/mecfs/is-long-covid-the-same-as-me-cfs/) walks through the overlap.

## How do you know if you are getting better?

Recovery from long COVID is rarely a straight line, so judging it by how you feel today is misleading. A bad week after a good month can feel like going backwards when the monthly trend is still moving the right way. [What Recovery Actually Looks Like](/insights/recovery/what-recovery-looks-like/) goes into why the curve is uneven.

A few habits make the trend visible:

1. **Measure the same way every time.** A morning heart rate variability (HRV) and resting heart rate reading, taken at the same time and position, is more useful than readings at random times.
2. **Repeat a stand test monthly.** If you have POTS-type symptoms, the rise in heart rate when you stand is one of the clearest measures of change.
3. **Count crash days.** Fewer crash days a month, or shorter crashes, is progress even if your best days feel the same.
4. **Compare 4 to 8 week averages.** Single days are noisy. Averages over weeks show whether your baseline is shifting.
5. **Log what changed.** Note new treatments, sleep, infections and big events, so you can tell a real trend from a one-off.

## When should you see a doctor?

See a clinician if your symptoms are getting steadily worse rather than up and down, if you develop new symptoms, or if you have never been properly assessed for POTS, ME/CFS or other conditions that can follow COVID. Get urgent care for chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath or any symptom that frightens you. If doctors have dismissed you before, bringing a few months of tracked data can make the conversation more concrete.

## The bottom line

Long COVID lasts months for many people and years for a smaller group, and most recovery tends to happen in the first year before slowing down. Severe fatigue and POTS-type symptoms are linked to slower recovery, and vaccination before infection to faster recovery, but none of that decides your own course. Pace to avoid repeated crashes, track the same few measures consistently and judge your progress over months, not days.
