If you have been sick since a COVID infection, someone has probably mentioned ME/CFS, and with it a quiet worry: is this the same illness, and does that change anything? **Long COVID and ME/CFS are not the same diagnosis, but they overlap heavily, and a share of people with long COVID meet the full criteria for ME/CFS.** Long COVID is a wide umbrella defined by its trigger. ME/CFS (myalgic encephalomyelitis / chronic fatigue syndrome) is a specific illness defined by its symptoms, above all post-exertional malaise. This article walks through how each one is defined, how many people with long COVID cross over, where the two differ, and how to tell which framework fits your own pattern.

These are educational notes, not medical advice. A diagnosis of either condition belongs with a clinician who has examined you and ruled out other causes.

## How long COVID and ME/CFS are each defined

The confusion starts with the definitions, because they are built in completely different ways.

**Long COVID is defined by what caused it and how long it lasts.** In 2024 the National Academies defined it as an infection-associated chronic condition that occurs after a SARS-CoV-2 infection and is present for at least three months, as a continuous, relapsing and remitting, or progressive disease state that affects one or more organ systems ([National Academies, 2024](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/2024/06/federal-government-clinicians-employers-and-others-should-adopt-new-definition-for-long-covid-to-aid-in-consistent-diagnosis-documentation-and-treatment)). It does not require a positive test, and it does not require any particular symptom. The same report lists conditions long COVID can include, and ME/CFS sits on that list alongside [POTS and other forms of dysautonomia](/insights/postviral/is-it-long-covid-or-pots/), mast cell activation syndrome and others.

**ME/CFS is defined by a specific set of symptoms, whatever started it.** Under the 2015 Institute of Medicine criteria, a person needs all three of these ([CDC, IOM 2015 criteria](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/diagnosis/iom-2015-diagnostic-criteria-1.html)):

1. A substantial drop in the ability to work, study or take part in life, lasting more than six months, with fatigue that is new, not caused by ongoing overexertion and not substantially relieved by rest.
2. Post-exertional malaise (PEM): symptoms get worse after physical, mental or emotional effort that would not have caused a problem before.
3. Unrefreshing sleep.

They also need at least one of cognitive impairment (brain fog) or orthostatic intolerance (symptoms that worsen upright and ease lying down). The criteria ask that PEM, unrefreshing sleep and cognitive problems be present at least half the time at moderate intensity or worse.

So the two labels answer different questions. Long COVID asks "did this follow COVID and has it lasted?" ME/CFS asks "does it look like this specific pattern?" One person can answer yes to both.

## Where long COVID and ME/CFS overlap

When researchers line the two illnesses up, the overlap is striking. A 2023 review by Komaroff and Lipkin found that both share fatigue, post-exertional malaise, cognitive problems, unrefreshing sleep, headaches, muscle and joint pain, and orthostatic intolerance, along with many less central symptoms such as palpitations and breathlessness ([Komaroff and Lipkin, 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2023.1187163)). The same review describes overlapping biological findings, including autonomic dysfunction, reduced blood flow to the brain, immune changes, reduced energy production and blood vessel dysfunction.

The autonomic overlap is especially relevant if you track your heart rate and HRV. A 2026 study from a Boston autonomic laboratory compared 143 people with long COVID and 170 with ME/CFS and found similar disturbances in both groups: abnormal blood flow to the brain on standing, impaired autonomic reflexes and small fiber nerve damage, with standard lab tests unable to tell the two groups apart ([Novak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0341278)). The authors were careful about what that means: it is still unclear whether the two are distinct but overlapping conditions or share one underlying pathway.

ME/CFS was also never a single-cause illness. The CDC notes that up to 80% of patients develop it after an acute viral-like illness, and that no single infectious agent has been established as the cause ([CDC, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)). Seen that way, COVID is the newest and largest of many infections that can leave this pattern behind. If you want the wider research picture, see our [2026 research roundup on long COVID and dysautonomia](/insights/research/long-covid-dysautonomia-research-2026/).

## How many people with long COVID meet ME/CFS criteria?

This is where the numbers can look contradictory, because the answer depends almost entirely on who is being counted.

The largest look comes from the NIH RECOVER-Adult study. Among 11,785 adults who had COVID and 1,439 who had not, each seen at least six months after infection, 4.5% of those infected met the 2015 ME/CFS criteria, compared with 0.6% of those never infected ([Vernon et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-024-09290-9)). New cases arose at 2.66 per 100 person-years after infection versus 0.93 in uninfected people, a hazard ratio of about 4.9. Nearly 9 in 10 of those with post-COVID ME/CFS (88.7%) also met RECOVER's long COVID criteria, so post-COVID ME/CFS almost always sat inside long COVID rather than beside it.

Look only at people who are still very unwell, and the share climbs steeply. In a German clinic study of 42 patients with persistent moderate to severe fatigue and exertion intolerance about six months after mild to moderate COVID, 19 (about 45%) met the stricter Canadian Consensus Criteria for ME/CFS ([Kedor et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-32507-6)). Most of those who did not qualify still had PEM, just lasting under the study's 14-hour threshold. Across studies of people with persistent, debilitating symptoms after COVID, Komaroff and Lipkin estimated that 13% to 45% meet the National Academy of Medicine definition of ME/CFS ([Komaroff and Lipkin, 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2023.1187163)).

For context, ME/CFS was not rare before the pandemic either. In 2021 to 2022, 1.3% of US adults reported having been diagnosed with ME/CFS and still having it, with women more affected than men (1.7% versus 0.9%), and the authors note that undiagnosed people are missed, so the true figure may be higher ([Vahratian et al., 2023](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db488.htm)). The CDC estimates that as many as 3.3 million Americans have ME/CFS and that the vast majority are undiagnosed ([CDC, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)).

## Where they differ

Overlap is not identity. A few differences show up consistently.

**Long COVID is broader.** Because it is defined by its trigger, long COVID includes people whose main problem is something ME/CFS does not cover at all, such as lung scarring, kidney disease or diabetes after infection, all of which the National Academies list as possible forms of long COVID ([National Academies, 2024](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/2024/06/federal-government-clinicians-employers-and-others-should-adopt-new-definition-for-long-covid-to-aid-in-consistent-diagnosis-documentation-and-treatment)). Someone with breathlessness from lung damage has long COVID but not ME/CFS.

**Some symptoms lean one way.** In the Komaroff and Lipkin review, loss of smell and taste, rash and hair loss were more frequent in long COVID, while painful lymph nodes, chemical sensitivities and tinnitus were more frequent in ME/CFS ([Komaroff and Lipkin, 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2023.1187163)).

**The timeline differs.** Long COVID can be named at three months. ME/CFS needs more than six months of reduced function, so a person can have long COVID that later meets ME/CFS criteria, or improves before it ever does.

**PEM itself may not look identical.** A Bateman Horne Center survey of 80 people seeking care for long COVID found that all but one reported PEM, but their triggers, symptoms during a crash and recovery strategies differed in several ways from 151 people with ME/CFS, while rest and pacing helped both groups ([Vernon et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.3233/WOR-220581)). That sample came from a specialist clinic, so it overstates how common PEM is across all long COVID.

## Why post-exertional malaise is the line that matters

If one feature decides whether the ME/CFS framework fits you, it is PEM. The CDC calls it a hallmark of ME/CFS: symptoms that worsen after physical, mental or emotional effort ([CDC, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)). It is different from ordinary tiredness because the worsening is out of proportion to what you did and is delayed: symptoms typically worsen 12 to 48 hours after the activity and can last days or even weeks ([CDC, Strategies to Prevent Worsening of Symptoms](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/clinical-care/treating-the-most-disruptive-symptoms-first-and-preventing-worsening-of-symptoms.html)). That lag makes the link easy to miss. Our guide to [post-exertional malaise](/insights/postviral/post-exertional-malaise-explained/) covers it in detail.

There is now physical evidence that PEM is not just a feeling. In a 2024 study of people with long COVID, muscle tissue examined after exercise that triggered PEM showed worsening metabolic disturbances, exercise-induced muscle damage and amyloid-containing deposits in the muscle tissue ([Appelman et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-44432-3)). The CDC warns that standard exercise recommendations for healthy people can substantially harm people with ME/CFS, and that pacing, balancing rest and activity, is how PEM is reduced ([CDC, Strategies to Prevent Worsening of Symptoms](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/clinical-care/treating-the-most-disruptive-symptoms-first-and-preventing-worsening-of-symptoms.html)). That is why the old advice to push through fatigue is risky for anyone with PEM, whichever label they carry.

<div class="callout callout-note"><strong>Exertion is not only exercise.</strong> A long phone call, a stressful appointment, a shower or a few hours of screen time can all count. If you only watch your workouts, you can miss the triggers that matter most.</div>

## Does the ME/CFS label change what you should do?

Practically, the label matters less than the pattern. If you have PEM, the most useful lessons from decades of ME/CFS care apply to you now, whether or not anyone has written ME/CFS in your chart.

| If this describes you | What it points toward |
| --- | --- |
| Symptoms flare a day or two after more activity | Pacing within an energy envelope rather than graded increases |
| Symptoms worse upright, better lying down | Checking for POTS or orthostatic intolerance with a stand test |
| Unrefreshing sleep and brain fog most days | Raising the ME/CFS criteria with your clinician |
| Main problem is in one organ (lungs, kidneys) | A long COVID workup aimed at that organ |

The label can still help. It points to clinicians who understand PEM, to a body of guidance on pacing, and it can carry weight in conversations about work and support. It also has limits: there is no lab test that confirms ME/CFS, and a self-assessment is not a diagnosis. Bring your pattern to a clinician and let them weigh it.

## How to tell which pattern fits you

The most useful evidence is your own, gathered over weeks. Three things are worth watching:

1. **The delayed flare.** Note what you did on a given day and how you felt one and two days later. A repeating lag between effort and worsening is the pattern PEM leaves, and our guide to [HRV before, during and after a PEM crash](/insights/hrv/hrv-and-pem-crash/) shows what it can look like in the numbers.
2. **Upright versus lying down.** If standing makes things worse and lying down helps, an at-home stand test can show whether your heart rate rise points toward POTS.
3. **Your activity ceiling.** Track roughly how much you can do without a crash. Learning that ceiling is the heart of [pacing and the energy envelope](/insights/recovery/pacing-101-energy-envelope/), and a rising ceiling over months is one of the clearest signs of improvement.

An app can make the pattern easier to see. Autonomic's pacing budget learns how much activity your body handled on days that did not end in a crash, which turns "how much can I do today?" into a number you can check rather than a guess. You can read more on the [ME/CFS page](/conditions/me-cfs/).

## The bottom line

Is long COVID the same as ME/CFS? No. Long COVID is an umbrella for any lasting illness after a COVID infection, and ME/CFS is a specific pattern defined by a big drop in function, unrefreshing sleep and post-exertional malaise lasting more than six months. But they overlap so much that, for many people, the distinction is mostly on paper. A few percent of everyone who has had COVID go on to meet ME/CFS criteria, and among those who stay seriously unwell the share can approach half. The feature to watch is PEM. If your symptoms flare after effort, take the ME/CFS lessons seriously: pace, protect your energy and avoid pushing through, and work with a clinician who knows the territory.
