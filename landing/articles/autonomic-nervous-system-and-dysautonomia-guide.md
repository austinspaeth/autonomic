Your heart races when you stand up, your head swims in a hot room, your gut stalls after meals and nobody can find anything wrong with your heart. Someone mentions "dysautonomia" and you are left wondering what that actually is. **Dysautonomia is a malfunction of the autonomic nervous system**, the part of your nervous system that runs heart rate, blood pressure, digestion, sweating and temperature without you deciding anything. It is not one disease but an umbrella term for several conditions, from common ones like POTS to rare, progressive ones ([Dysautonomia International](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/page.php?ID=34)). This guide covers how the system works, the types of dysautonomia, the symptoms, the causes, how it is tested and treated, and what you can measure yourself.

## How does the autonomic nervous system work?

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is the automatic control layer of your body. Right now it is adjusting your heart rate beat by beat, tightening and relaxing blood vessels to keep blood pressure steady as you shift position, moving food along your gut and setting how much you sweat. It has two main branches that pull in opposite directions, and health is the two handing off smoothly to each other.

- The **sympathetic** branch is the accelerator ("fight or flight"). It speeds the heart, tightens blood vessels and raises blood pressure. It is what keeps blood reaching your brain when you stand up.
- The **parasympathetic** branch is the brake ("rest and digest"), carried largely by the **vagus nerve**. It slows the heart and supports digestion and recovery.

A third, often forgotten piece is the **sudomotor** system, the sympathetic nerves that control sweating. Autonomic testing checks all three: the blood pressure (adrenergic) response, the heart-rate (cardiovagal) response and sweating ([Cheshire et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2020.11.024)).

## What is dysautonomia?

Dysautonomia is the umbrella term for any condition in which the ANS fails to regulate properly. Cleveland Clinic and Dysautonomia International both estimate that more than 70 million people worldwide live with some form of it ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/6004-dysautonomia); [Dysautonomia International](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/page.php?ID=34)). It can be mild or severe.

Clinicians split it two ways:

- **Primary** dysautonomia happens on its own. It includes inherited forms such as familial dysautonomia and idiopathic forms, where no cause is found.
- **Secondary** dysautonomia is caused by another condition, such as diabetes, Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, lupus or certain medications ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/6004-dysautonomia)).

<div class="callout callout-note"><strong>Dysautonomia is not the same as "dysregulation."</strong> Dysautonomia is a medical diagnosis made with testing. "Nervous system dysregulation" is a looser everyday phrase for feeling stuck on high alert. If that second one sounds more like you, read <a href="/insights/basics/nervous-system-dysregulation-signs-and-recovery/">our page on nervous system dysregulation</a>.</div>

## What are the types of dysautonomia?

The types differ in which part of the system fails, how they are diagnosed and how they behave over time. These are the main ones.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Type | What goes wrong | Good to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [POTS](/insights/pots/how-pots-is-diagnosed/) (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome) | Heart rate rises at least 30 bpm on standing (40 bpm at ages 12 to 19), with symptoms and no fall in blood pressure | The best-known type; some people develop it after COVID-19 |
| [Orthostatic hypotension](/insights/pots/orthostatic-hypotension-vs-pots/) | Blood pressure drops at least 20 systolic or 10 diastolic within 3 minutes of standing | Can come from autonomic nerve failure or from other causes, which is why it needs a workup |
| [Vasovagal (neurocardiogenic) syncope](/insights/basics/vasovagal-syncope-fainting/) | A reflex suddenly drops heart rate and blood pressure, causing a faint | Very common; by age 60, 42% of women and 32% of men have fainted this way at least once |
| [Inappropriate sinus tachycardia](/insights/pots/inappropriate-sinus-tachycardia-vs-pots/) | Resting heart rate of 100 bpm or more, averaging 90 bpm or more over 24 hours, with distressing palpitations | Fast all the time, not mainly on standing, which separates it from POTS |
| Autonomic neuropathy | Damage to the small autonomic nerves, as in diabetic neuropathy or [small fiber neuropathy](/insights/pots/small-fiber-neuropathy-pots-long-covid/) | An estimated 20% of people with diabetes develop cardiac autonomic neuropathy |
| Autoimmune autonomic ganglionopathy (AAG) | Antibodies attack the receptor that relays signals through autonomic nerve junctions | Rare; can begin after an infection; often causes low blood pressure on standing, constipation and dry eyes or mouth |
| Pure autonomic failure | Progressive failure of autonomic nerves, with blood pressure falling on standing | In one cohort, about a third developed Parkinson's, Lewy body dementia or MSA within 4 years |
| Multiple system atrophy (MSA) | A progressive brain disease, related to Parkinson's, that causes autonomic failure | Rare and progressive; an estimated 350,000 people worldwide |
| Familial dysautonomia | An inherited disorder (ELP1 gene) affecting autonomic and sensory nerve development | Affects about 1 in 3,700 people of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry |

</div>

Sources for the table: POTS, inappropriate sinus tachycardia and vasovagal syncope criteria from the Heart Rhythm Society consensus statement ([Sheldon et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hrthm.2015.03.029)); orthostatic hypotension from the standard consensus criteria as summarised by [van Twist et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10286-021-00833-2); diabetes figure from [Dysautonomia International](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/page.php?ID=150); AAG from [Nakane et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms25042296); pure autonomic failure from [Kaufmann et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.24877); MSA from [Dysautonomia International](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/page.php?ID=34); familial dysautonomia from [MedlinePlus Genetics](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/familial-dysautonomia).

Most readers of this site are dealing with the first four, which are functional problems in nerves that are still present. The last four involve damaged or degenerating nerves and are much rarer.

## What are the symptoms of dysautonomia?

Because the ANS touches nearly every organ, symptoms scatter across the body and can look unrelated until you see the common thread. Cleveland Clinic lists dizziness and fainting (especially on standing), a fast or slow heart rate, digestive and bladder changes, unusual sweating, fatigue, brain fog, vision changes, sleep problems and anxiety among the common ones ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/6004-dysautonomia)).

| Function | What the ANS does | What dysregulation can feel like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heart rate | Matches beats to posture and effort | Racing heart, palpitations, tachycardia on standing |
| Blood pressure | Holds pressure steady as you move | Dizziness, lightheadedness, fainting |
| Digestion | Drives gut movement | Nausea, bloating, early fullness, constipation |
| Sweating and temperature | Controls sweat and skin blood flow | Heat intolerance, too much or too little sweat, cold hands and feet |
| Blood flow to the brain | Keeps it steady when upright | Brain fog, trouble concentrating, blurred vision |
| Bladder and pupils | Background control | Urgency or retention, light sensitivity |

<div class="callout callout-note"><strong>The pattern is the clue.</strong> No single symptom here is unique to dysautonomia. What points to an autonomic cause is the cluster: a racing heart on standing plus fatigue, brain fog and gut trouble, all worse when you are upright.</div>

## What causes dysautonomia?

Often there is a clear trigger or underlying condition. Just as often there is not, and Dysautonomia International notes that some people never find a specific cause ([Dysautonomia International](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/page.php?ID=150)). The main known causes:

- **After a viral illness.** Some people with long COVID have symptoms and findings that meet the criteria for POTS. The American Autonomic Society treats the mechanism as an open question, with inflammation, autoimmunity and blood volume changes among the candidates ([Raj et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10286-021-00798-2)). We cover how this overlaps with mast cell problems in [POTS, long COVID and MCAS](/insights/postviral/pots-long-covid-and-mcas-overlap/).
- **Autoimmune disease.** AAG is directly caused by antibodies, and its sudden or subacute onset often follows a flu-like illness or gut infection ([Nakane et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms25042296)). Conditions such as Sjogren's, lupus and rheumatoid arthritis can also cause secondary dysautonomia ([Dysautonomia International](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/page.php?ID=34)). See [Sjogren's and dysautonomia](/insights/pots/sjogrens-dysautonomia-pots/).
- **Diabetes.** Diabetes can damage autonomic nerves, and an estimated 20% of people with diabetes develop cardiac autonomic neuropathy ([Dysautonomia International](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/page.php?ID=150)).
- **Hypermobility and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome.** EDS, especially the hypermobile type, appears to be common in people with autonomic dysfunction ([Dysautonomia International](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/page.php?ID=150)). More in [hypermobility, EDS and POTS](/insights/pots/hypermobility-eds-and-pots/).
- **Genetics.** Familial dysautonomia is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern: a person needs two changed copies of the ELP1 gene ([MedlinePlus Genetics](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/familial-dysautonomia)).
- **Medications and toxins.** Some medicines and toxins can cause or worsen autonomic symptoms ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/6004-dysautonomia)). If you suspect one of yours, raise it with the prescriber rather than stopping it yourself.
- **Neurodegenerative disease.** Parkinson's disease, Lewy body dementia and MSA can all cause autonomic failure. Pure autonomic failure can be the first sign of one of them ([Kaufmann et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.24877)).

Deconditioning is not usually the root cause, but it can make symptoms worse, which is one reason reconditioning is part of treatment ([Dysautonomia International](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/page.php?ID=150)).

## How is dysautonomia diagnosed?

Diagnosis is partly a process of elimination: a clinician looks for a pattern of symptoms and rules out other explanations ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/6004-dysautonomia)). The international consensus on autonomic testing is clear that "no single test alone is sufficient" to judge how much of the system is affected or where, so specialists combine tests from the three branches ([Cheshire et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2020.11.024)).

| Test | What it checks | Branch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Tilt table test](/insights/pots/tilt-table-test-what-to-expect/) or active stand | Heart rate and blood pressure while upright | Sympathetic (adrenergic) |
| Valsalva maneuver | Blood pressure and heart rate while you blow against resistance | Adrenergic and cardiovagal |
| Deep breathing test | How much your heart rate swings with slow, deep breaths | Parasympathetic (cardiovagal) |
| QSART and thermoregulatory sweat test | Whether the sweat nerves respond normally | Sudomotor |
| Skin biopsy | Density of small nerve fibers in the skin | Small fiber nerves |
| Blood tests and ECG | Anemia, thyroid problems, heart rhythm | Rules out other causes |

A few details worth knowing before you go in:

- **Tilt testing** is the standard test for orthostatic hypotension and POTS, with beat-to-beat heart rate and blood pressure recorded while you are tilted upright ([Cheshire et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2020.11.024)). For POTS, the Heart Rhythm Society also recommends a full history, exam, orthostatic vital signs and a 12-lead ECG, with a blood count and thyroid tests in selected patients ([Sheldon et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hrthm.2015.03.029)). Our guide to [how POTS is diagnosed](/insights/pots/how-pots-is-diagnosed/) walks through the numbers.
- **The deep breathing test** is the most widely used clinical test of the parasympathetic side ([Cheshire et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2020.11.024)).
- **QSART** delivers a small amount of a sweat-triggering chemical through the skin to test the sweat nerves directly ([Cheshire et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinph.2020.11.024)).
- **Skin biopsy** for small fiber neuropathy is a 3 mm punch taken from the lower leg, about 10 cm above the ankle, with the nerve fiber count compared to age-adjusted norms ([Lauria et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-1331.2010.03023.x)).
- **Antibody tests** can support a diagnosis of AAG, and Cleveland Clinic notes that bloodwork may also look at neurotransmitter levels ([Nakane et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms25042296); [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/6004-dysautonomia)).

For what each appointment feels like, see [autonomic function tests: what to expect](/insights/pots/autonomic-function-tests-what-to-expect/).

## How is dysautonomia treated?

There is no single cure for dysautonomia, but many symptoms can be managed, and the plan depends on the type ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/6004-dysautonomia)). When dysautonomia is secondary to another disease, treating that disease matters, and secondary forms may improve when it is treated ([Dysautonomia International](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/page.php?ID=34)).

For POTS, the best-studied functional type, the Heart Rhythm Society says non-drug measures "should be attempted first with all patients" ([Sheldon et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hrthm.2015.03.029)):

- **Fluids and salt.** For people with low blood volume, the statement suggests 2 to 3 litres of water a day and, if tolerated, 10 to 12 g of salt a day. Salt is not right for everyone, especially with high blood pressure or kidney or heart disease, so agree it with your clinician first.
- **Compression** garments to reduce blood pooling.
- **Reconditioning.** A regular, structured, progressive exercise program can be effective, starting with exercise that is not upright, such as rowing, recumbent cycling or swimming.
- **Medications.** Several exist, and the statement lists options for different patterns of POTS. Which one, if any, is a decision for your clinician, made around your blood pressure, heart rate and other conditions.

For autoimmune forms, immunotherapy is used and can help ([Nakane et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms25042296); [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/6004-dysautonomia)). Neurogenic orthostatic hypotension and the degenerative types are managed by specialists with a different set of tools. Our deeper guide covers [salt, fluids, compression and medication for POTS](/insights/pots/pots-treatment-salt-fluids-compression-medication/).

<div class="callout callout-tip"><strong>Bring numbers, not just a list of symptoms.</strong> A few weeks of morning heart rate, HRV and home stand-test results gives your clinician a starting point that a single office visit cannot.</div>

## Does dysautonomia go away?

It depends on the type, and anyone who gives you one answer for all of them is oversimplifying.

- **Vasovagal syncope** is common, and many people faint only once or twice in their lives ([Sheldon et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hrthm.2015.03.029)).
- **POTS** is thought to be chronic but to improve eventually, and the Heart Rhythm Society notes that "its course probably varies substantially from patient to patient," with long-term outcomes poorly studied ([Sheldon et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hrthm.2015.03.029)). See [does POTS get better over time?](/insights/pots/does-pots-get-better-over-time/)
- **Secondary dysautonomia** may improve when the underlying disease is treated ([Dysautonomia International](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/page.php?ID=34)).
- **Pure autonomic failure** carries a high risk of turning into a brain disease: in a US cohort, 25 of 74 people (34%) developed Lewy body dementia, Parkinson's or MSA within 4 years ([Kaufmann et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.24877)).
- **MSA** is progressive, and Dysautonomia International describes it as fatal, usually within 5 to 10 years ([Dysautonomia International](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/page.php?ID=34)).

Cleveland Clinic sums it up honestly: the outlook is unpredictable, some forms are temporary, many are lifelong, and some people go months or years without symptoms ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/6004-dysautonomia)).

- **Heart rate variability (HRV).** RMSSD, the main short-term HRV measure, is used to estimate vagally mediated, parasympathetic changes in heart rate ([Shaffer and Ginsberg, 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2017.00258)). Read more in [the complete HRV guide](/insights/hrv/hrv-complete-guide/).
- **Resting heart rate.** Your morning resting heart rate, taken the same way each day, shows when your system is under more load than usual. A rise that holds for several days deserves a note next to your symptoms.
- **The stand test.** The heart-rate rise from lying to standing is the number that defines POTS. Here is [how to run a stand test at home](/insights/pots/the-orthostatic-stand-test-at-home/).

None of these diagnoses dysautonomia, and a home number is not a tilt table. But measured the same way each morning and read as trends, they turn an invisible condition into something you and your clinician can follow.

## The bottom line

Dysautonomia is an umbrella term for conditions in which the autonomic nervous system does not regulate heart rate, blood pressure, digestion, sweating and temperature properly. It covers common functional problems such as POTS, orthostatic hypotension, vasovagal syncope and inappropriate sinus tachycardia, nerve-damage types such as diabetic and small fiber neuropathy, and rare autoimmune, inherited and degenerative disorders. Causes range from viral illness, autoimmunity, diabetes and hypermobility to genetics, and are sometimes never found. Diagnosis combines several tests because no single one is enough, treatment depends on the type, and the outlook ranges from improving over time to progressive. Knowing which type you have is the most useful step.

Do not start, stop or change a medication or your salt intake without your clinician. If your symptoms are new, worsening or frightening, or you faint, get medical care.</div>
