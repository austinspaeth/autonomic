## Surgery with POTS is very doable, with a little planning

If you have POTS and a surgery or procedure on the calendar, the fear is understandable. You have spent a long time learning that standing up, skipping fluids, getting overheated and sudden position changes all make you feel terrible, and surgery seems to involve all of them at once. Here is the reassuring part, stated plainly: people with POTS have operations safely all the time, and anesthesiologists are comfortable managing you once they know what they are working with.

The trouble, when there is trouble, almost never comes from the surgery itself. It comes from a short list of predictable perioperative triggers that stack on top of each other, combined with a care team that was not told you have POTS. This article walks through why the perioperative period is hard on a POTS body, what to arrange before the day, what to ask your anesthesiologist, and how to pace the recovery so it does not become a flare that outlasts the incision.

## Why the perioperative period is hard on POTS

POTS is, at its core, a problem of keeping enough blood circulating to your brain when you are upright. Many people with POTS run on a lower-than-average blood volume and have blood vessels that are slow to clamp down when gravity pulls blood into the legs and abdomen. The autonomic system compensates by driving the heart rate up. Surgery pokes at every part of that system.

**Fasting and dehydration shrink an already small tank.** You are told not to eat or drink for hours before surgery, often from midnight. For most people that is a minor inconvenience. For someone whose POTS is partly driven by [low blood volume](/insights/pots/low-blood-volume-pots-hypovolemia/), an overnight fast on top of normal morning dehydration can meaningfully reduce circulating volume before you even arrive, which makes the heart-rate spike and lightheadedness worse all day.

**Lying flat, then sitting up, is the stand test from hell.** During surgery you are horizontal for a long stretch, which is actually comfortable for a POTS body because gravity is off. The problem is the transition out. Being raised upright in the recovery room, after fluid shifts, blood loss and medications, can produce a large and abrupt heart-rate rise exactly when you are groggy and cannot brace for it.

**Anesthetic drugs often relax blood vessels.** Many general and sedative agents, and spinal or epidural blocks, cause vasodilation: the blood vessels widen. In a typical patient the body compensates. In someone who already pools blood and runs low on volume, that widening can drop blood pressure further, so anesthesiologists plan for it with fluids and, when needed, medications that tighten the vessels back up.

**Temperature control is already shaky.** Operating rooms are cold, anesthesia impairs your ability to regulate temperature, and many people with POTS have [poor temperature regulation](/insights/pots/heat-intolerance-and-pots/) to begin with. Getting cold worsens everything and slows recovery, so active warming matters more for you than for the average patient.

**Held medications leave a gap.** If you take a beta blocker, ivabradine, midodrine or fludrocortisone and it gets paused around surgery, your usual rate and blood-pressure control is missing at the exact moment the system is stressed.

## What to do before the day: the pre-op plan

The best perioperative outcome in POTS is bought weeks earlier, at the pre-operative visit, not improvised on the morning of surgery. The goal is simple: no one in the operating or recovery room should be surprised that you have POTS, and a fast heart rate should be read as your known pattern rather than a new emergency.

### Make sure the whole team knows, in writing

Tell the surgeon, the pre-op nurse and especially the anesthesiologist that you have POTS, and get it into the chart. If you can, ask for a brief anesthesia consultation before the day of surgery so the plan is made with time to spare. Bring a one-page summary: your diagnosis, your typical resting versus standing heart rate, your medications and supplements, what reliably triggers you, and what has helped in past procedures.

### Bring an accurate medication and supplement list

This is where people get caught out. List everything, including the doses and the last time you took each: beta blockers, ivabradine, midodrine, fludrocortisone, pyridostigmine, clonidine or guanfacine, salt tablets, electrolyte mixes, antihistamines if you have [MCAS](/insights/postviral/pots-long-covid-and-mcas-overlap/), and any supplement that affects bleeding or heart rate. Crucially, ask which to take with a sip of water on the morning of surgery and which to hold. Do not guess. Stopping a beta blocker abruptly, or doubling up, both cause problems, and the right answer depends on the drug and the operation.

### Ask about fluids and timing

Two of the most useful requests cost almost nothing. First, ask whether you can have a longer window for clear fluids before surgery, since many centers now allow clear liquids up to two hours before anesthesia, which is kinder to a low-volume body than a hard midnight cutoff. Second, ask about receiving IV fluids before induction. A liter or more of saline given before you go under is a common, low-risk way to counter the fast and blunt the heart-rate spike afterward. Both are the anesthesiologist's call, informed by your heart and kidney health, but they are entirely reasonable to raise in advance.

### Pack for a POTS recovery

Bring your compression garments and plan to put them on as early as the team allows, because the same [abdominal and waist-high compression](/insights/pots/compression-garments-for-pots/) that helps you stand at home helps you sit up in recovery. Bring your own electrolyte supply for once you are cleared to drink, loose warm layers, and sunglasses or an eye mask if light sensitivity is part of your picture.

## Questions worth asking your anesthesiologist

You do not need to tell the specialist how to do their job. You need to surface the POTS-specific levers so they can decide. A short, specific list works better than a general worry.

Beyond the checklist, it is fair to ask how they plan to handle blood-pressure drops (usually fluids first, then a vasoconstrictor if needed), whether regional or general anesthesia is being considered and why, and what the recovery-room plan is for your rate. If you have reacted badly to a specific drug before, say so clearly.

## During surgery: what the team controls

Most of this is out of your hands once you are asleep, which is exactly why the planning matters. For your own understanding, here is what a POTS-aware team tends to do.

| Perioperative challenge | Why it hits POTS harder | What tends to help |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overnight fast and dehydration | Low blood volume gets lower | Clear fluids until 2h before where allowed, IV fluids before induction |
| Vasodilating anesthetics | Widened vessels drop pressure in a pooling body | Fluid loading, careful dosing, vasoconstrictor on hand |
| Spinal or epidural block | Can cause a larger blood-pressure drop | Pre-loading fluids, slow dosing, close monitoring |
| Cold operating room | Impaired temperature control worsens symptoms | Forced-air warming blanket, warmed IV fluids |
| Moving from flat to upright | The classic orthostatic heart-rate spike | Raise the head of the bed gradually, compression on, fluids in |
| Pain and nausea afterward | Stress and vomiting worsen volume and rate | Good pain and anti-nausea control, which protect your volume |

Two details are worth knowing. There is no anesthetic drug that is universally forbidden in POTS, and general, regional and local techniques are all used successfully; the specialist chooses based on the operation and on you. And because blood loss and fluid shifts matter more when your reserve is small, POTS-aware teams tend to keep you a little more topped up than average and to correct nausea aggressively, since vomiting is a fast way to lose the volume they just gave you.

## Recovering without triggering a crash

Here is the part most patients underestimate. The operation ends, but the POTS flare is often just beginning, and it can run for one to several weeks. Anesthesia, pain, less movement, interrupted sleep and a few days of lighter fluid intake all push your orthostatic symptoms up at once. The move is to expect the flare and pace through it, not to prove how fast you can bounce back.

- **Rebuild upright time in small steps.** Treat the first days like starting a [stand-test](/insights/pots/the-orthostatic-stand-test-at-home/) protocol from scratch: sit before you stand, stand before you walk, and stop while you still feel fine rather than when you feel bad.
- **Get fluids and salt back up the moment you are cleared to drink.** The same [salt and fluid](/insights/pots/pots-treatment-salt-fluids-compression-medication/) measures that steady you day to day matter double in the week after surgery.
- **Restart your POTS medications on your team's schedule,** not your own guess, and ask specifically when each one resumes.
- **Keep compression on** during upright time while you rebuild tolerance.
- **Guard against post-exertional malaise** if you also have long COVID or ME/CFS. The temptation to catch up on everything the day you feel slightly better is exactly how people trigger [post-exertional malaise](/insights/postviral/post-exertional-malaise-explained/). Plan to do less than you think you can, and read our [pacing guide](/insights/recovery/pacing-101-energy-envelope/) before you overdo it.

If your resting heart rate stays unusually high for days, you feel faint repeatedly even lying down, you have calf pain or swelling, chest pain, shortness of breath that is new or severe, a fever, or any sign of wound infection, those are reasons to call your surgical team rather than wait it out. A POTS flare and a surgical complication can feel similar, so when in doubt, ask.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it safe to have surgery with POTS?** Yes. The large majority of people with POTS have surgery without serious problems. What makes it smoother is planning for the predictable triggers (the fast and dehydration, lying flat then sitting up, and vessel-relaxing drugs) and making sure the whole team knows the diagnosis in advance.

**What should I tell my anesthesiologist?** That you have POTS, roughly what your heart rate does lying versus standing, and every medication and supplement you take including the ones you were told to hold. Ask about IV fluids before induction, warming, and slow repositioning afterward.

**Do I need extra IV fluids?** Many people with POTS benefit from generous fluids around surgery because low blood volume is part of the condition and the fast makes it worse. Pre-operative IV fluids are a common, low-risk thing to ask about, decided by your anesthesiologist.

**Which anesthesia drugs are a problem?** No single drug is banned. The issue is that several agents relax blood vessels or blunt the heart-rate response, which anesthesiologists manage routinely with fluids and careful dosing. Spinal and epidural blocks can cause a larger blood-pressure drop in POTS, so they are planned for, not necessarily avoided.

**How long is recovery?** Expect your usual recovery plus a POTS flare lasting one to several weeks. Rebuild upright time gradually, keep fluids and salt up, use compression, and pace hard, especially if you also have long COVID or ME/CFS.

## The bottom line

Surgery with POTS is manageable, and the leverage is almost entirely in the preparation. The operating room is not where things go wrong; the stacked triggers around it are, and every one of them has a simple countermeasure. Make sure the whole team knows you have POTS, bring an accurate medication list and your own numbers, ask about fluids and warming and slow transfers, and then treat the recovery as a pacing problem rather than a sprint. Do that, and the hardest part of the day is usually the waiting.
