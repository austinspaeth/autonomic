## When your head is part of the dysautonomia

Ask a room full of people with POTS or dysautonomia what bothers them most, and headaches come up again and again, usually right after the dizziness and the fatigue. Yet **POTS headaches** and **dysautonomia headaches** rarely get their own line in a clinic note. They get folded into "stress," or treated as a separate migraine problem, or waved off as not drinking enough water. In reality they usually come from the same autonomic plumbing that sets your heart rate and moves your blood, and once you understand that, they stop being a mystery and start being something you can read.

This is educational field notes, not medical advice. The goal is to explain what is actually happening inside your head, give you a way to tell the main headache types apart, keep the genuine red flags in view, and show you how to track the pattern so you can see what helps.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <strong>The short version.</strong> Most headaches in POTS and dysautonomia come from being upright: blood pools low, cerebral blood flow drops, and your head and neck feel it, producing an orthostatic headache that eases when you lie down. Migraine is also genuinely more common in this group, and the two often coexist. The single most useful question is whether your headache tracks posture, because a posture-locked headache responds to fluids, salt, compression and pacing, while a migraine needs migraine-specific care. A brand-new, severe, or thunderclap headache, one that is worse lying down, or one with fever, vision loss or weakness needs prompt medical attention.

## Why does POTS and dysautonomia cause headaches?

Your brain is fussy about blood flow. It sits at the top of your body, above the pump, and it depends on a steady supply that your circulation is supposed to keep constant no matter what posture you are in. In POTS and orthostatic intolerance, that steadiness breaks down.

- **Standing pools your blood low.** When you are upright, gravity pulls a real share of your blood into your legs and the [splanchnic bed in your belly](/insights/pots/blood-pooling-in-pots-legs/). Less returns to your heart, which is the core problem POTS compensates for with a racing pulse.
- **Cerebral blood flow falls.** Studies of orthostatic intolerance consistently show reduced blood flow to the brain on standing, sometimes even when blood pressure reads normal. The brain is being underperfused while you are upright, and underperfused tissue aches. This is the most likely engine behind the orthostatic headache, and it overlaps with the mechanism behind [brain fog](/insights/postviral/long-covid-brain-fog/) and lightheadedness.
- **The postural muscles underperfuse too.** The muscles that hold your head and shoulders up are also short on blood flow while you stand, which produces the specific band of neck and shoulder ache called [coat hanger pain](/insights/pots/coat-hanger-pain-pots-orthostatic-intolerance/). It is a close cousin of the headache and often shows up with it.
- **Low blood volume makes it worse.** Many people with POTS run on [reduced plasma volume](/insights/pots/low-blood-volume-pots-hypovolemia/), so there is less circulating fluid to keep the head supplied in the first place, and any extra pooling bites harder.
- **The adrenaline response has a say.** The sympathetic surges that drive the tremor and the pounding heart also play into headache. In hyperadrenergic patterns a flare or an [adrenaline dump](/insights/pots/adrenaline-dumps-pots/) can arrive with its own pounding head.

On top of all that, there is migraine, which deserves its own section below, because it is not just a POTS symptom. It is a separate, common condition that happens to share a lot of wiring with the autonomic nervous system.

## The main headache types in POTS, side by side

Most people with dysautonomia do not have one headache. They have a couple of different ones that show up in different situations, and the mistake is treating them all the same. Here is how the common types compare.

| Headache type | What it feels like | Posture effect | What tends to help |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Orthostatic headache** | Dull, pressing, builds over minutes to hours of being upright | Worse upright, eases within minutes of lying down | Fluids, salt (with clinician), compression, pacing upright time, lying down early |
| **Migraine** | Throbbing, often one-sided, with nausea, light and sound sensitivity, sometimes aura | Little to no posture effect, lasts hours to days | Migraine-specific acute and preventive care, trigger control |
| **Coat hanger pain** | Band of ache across the base of the skull and tops of the shoulders | Worse upright, eases lying down | Same orthostatic toolkit, position changes, head support |
| **Tension-type** | Tight band around the head, mild to moderate, both sides | Not really posture-linked | Stress and sleep management, posture, gentle movement |
| **Cervicogenic** | Starts in the neck, spreads to one side of the head | Linked to neck position and movement, not standing | Neck care, physical therapy, clinician assessment |
| **Medication-overuse** | Daily or near-daily dull headache, often worse in the morning | No consistent posture link | Reducing frequent painkiller use, guided by a clinician |

The table is a starting point, not a diagnosis. Real headaches blur the lines, and you can absolutely have an orthostatic headache that then triggers a migraine on a bad day. The value is in asking the right questions: when did it start, what was I doing, did lying down change it, and what came with it.

## Orthostatic headache: the one that tracks standing

If your headache builds the longer you are on your feet and fades once you lie down, you are most likely dealing with an **orthostatic headache**. It is the head-pain version of everything else POTS does to you when you stand, and it responds to the same levers. People describe it as a pressure or a heaviness more than a throb, often across the forehead or the whole head, and it is frequently worse in the second half of the day when you have been upright for hours and your volume is running low.

This is the pattern that overlaps with [why POTS symptoms are worse in the morning](/insights/pots/pots-symptoms-worse-in-the-morning/) and across a long upright day. It is also the pattern that makes the posture question so useful, because the relief on lying down is a real, repeatable signal rather than a guess.

One important caution lives inside this type. A spontaneous cerebrospinal fluid leak produces the same positional headache, worse upright and better lying down, and it is more common in people with the connective tissue traits that travel with [hypermobility and EDS](/insights/pots/hypermobility-eds-and-pots/). A long-standing, mild orthostatic headache that behaves like the rest of your POTS is usually just the dysautonomia. A new, severe, or steadily worsening one, especially if it took hours to days to appear and now dominates your day, deserves a clinician's eval to rule a leak in or out.

## Migraine and POTS: two problems that travel together

Migraine is not caused by POTS, but it keeps POTS company. Studies consistently find migraine is more common in people with POTS than in the general population, often reported in roughly a third or more of patients. There are a few reasons this overlap is real and not just coincidence.

Migraine is, at its core, a disorder with heavy **autonomic involvement**. During an attack many people get cranial autonomic features: a watery eye, a stuffy or runny nose, facial flushing or sweating on one side. That is the same autonomic nervous system that is dysregulated in POTS, drawing on the trigeminovascular system and signaling molecules like CGRP. Layer on the fact that POTS most often affects younger women, the group with the highest background migraine rates anyway, and the two conditions stack.

The practical consequence is that hydration and salt will not fix a migraine. If your headache throbs, sits on one side, brings nausea and light sensitivity, and lasts for hours regardless of what posture you take, that is migraine territory, and it needs migraine-specific treatment planned with a clinician. Some of those treatments overlap usefully with POTS care, and some need weighing against your circulation, which is exactly the kind of decision to make with a prescriber rather than alone.

## What sets these headaches off

Headaches in dysautonomia rarely come from nowhere. They cluster around the same things that destabilize the rest of your autonomic state, which is good news, because most of them are trackable and some are adjustable.

- **Dehydration and low volume.** The fastest way to tip an orthostatic headache over the edge. Covered in [dehydration and HRV](/insights/hrv/dehydration-and-hrv/).
- **Heat and hot showers.** Heat widens your blood vessels and worsens pooling, which is why a hot day or a long hot shower can bring on both dizziness and a headache.
- **Weather and pressure swings.** Many people are sensitive to [barometric pressure changes](/insights/pots/barometric-pressure-weather-changes-pots/), a classic migraine trigger that also seems to nudge autonomic symptoms.
- **Caffeine, both ways.** A little can help by nudging blood pressure and constricting vessels, but inconsistent intake and withdrawal are strong headache triggers. See [caffeine and POTS](/insights/pots/caffeine-and-pots-heart-rate-hrv/).
- **Poor or broken sleep.** A short or fragmented night lowers your threshold for both headache types, and POTS makes good sleep hard to begin with, as in [POTS insomnia](/insights/pots/pots-insomnia-wired-but-tired/).
- **Skipped meals and blood sugar swings.** Going too long without eating, or a reactive glucose crash, can trigger a headache on top of the shakiness.
- **Hormones.** Menstrual timing shifts both migraine and autonomic symptoms for many people.
- **Overdoing it.** Pushing past your energy envelope invites a crash, and headache is often part of that crash, tangled up with [post-exertional malaise](/insights/postviral/post-exertional-malaise-explained/).

## What helps

Because these headaches come from more than one source, the fix is to treat the right one. The posture question above is how you decide where to start, and almost everything below is worth planning with the clinician who manages your condition.

- **Shore up your volume.** Steady fluids and, if your clinician agrees, [salt](/insights/pots/science-of-salt-and-fluids-for-pots/) keep more blood in circulation so your head is better supplied when you stand. This is first-line for the orthostatic type.
- **Use compression and pacing.** Compression garments blunt the pooling, and pacing your upright time, so you are not standing still for long stretches, keeps the orthostatic headache from building. Lying down early, before it peaks, works better than pushing through.
- **Be consistent with caffeine and meals.** Pick a steady caffeine routine rather than swinging, and avoid long gaps without food. Consistency matters more than the exact amount.
- **Protect sleep.** A regular sleep window and the usual POTS sleep measures lower your threshold for both headache types.
- **Consider magnesium with your clinician.** [Magnesium](/insights/food/magnesium-hrv-pots/) has a reasonable evidence base in migraine prevention and is often discussed in POTS. It is not a cure, and it is a conversation to have with a prescriber.
- **Treat migraine as migraine.** If the throbbing, one-sided, nausea-and-light-sensitivity pattern is yours, ask about acute and preventive migraine treatment specifically. Hydration will not touch it.
- **Track triggers.** A simple log of what preceded each headache turns a vague "I get headaches" into a short list you can actually act on, as in [finding your triggers with a symptom journal](/insights/recovery/find-your-triggers-symptom-journal/).

## Red flags: when a headache needs prompt care

Most headaches in POTS and dysautonomia are uncomfortable but not dangerous. A few patterns are different, and they are worth memorizing so you do not talk yourself out of being seen.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does POTS cause headaches?** Mostly because of where your blood is. Standing lets blood pool in your legs and belly, so less returns to your heart and brain, and studies consistently show reduced cerebral blood flow on standing in orthostatic intolerance, sometimes even when blood pressure looks normal. That underperfusion is a plausible driver of the headache, the brain fog and the lightheadedness, and it is why these headaches build with upright time and ease when you lie down. Migraine is also more common in POTS, so many people carry both.

**What is an orthostatic headache?** One that is brought on or worsened by being upright and that eases within minutes of lying down. In POTS it is usually tied to reduced blood flow to the head while you stand. It shares its positional fingerprint with a spontaneous cerebrospinal fluid leak, which is why a new, severe or worsening orthostatic headache is worth a clinician's evaluation rather than assuming it is only the dysautonomia.

**Are migraines common in POTS?** Yes. Studies consistently find migraine is more common in people with POTS than in the general population, often reported in roughly a third or more of patients. Migraine itself involves the autonomic nervous system, with cranial autonomic features during attacks, and POTS tends to affect younger women, the group with the highest background migraine rates. POTS does not cause migraine, but the two travel together often enough to screen for both.

**How can I tell a POTS headache from a migraine?** Watch what posture does. An orthostatic headache builds with upright time and eases within a few minutes of lying flat. A migraine throbs, often on one side, with nausea and light or sound sensitivity, lasts hours to days regardless of position, and may come with an aura. A tension-type headache is a tight band that does not care much about posture either. Many people with POTS have more than one kind, so logging timing, triggers and relief is the most reliable way to separate them.

**When is a headache a red flag rather than dysautonomia?** Get urgent care for a thunderclap headache that peaks within seconds, the worst headache of your life, a headache with fever and a stiff neck, new weakness, numbness, slurred speech or vision loss, a headache after a head injury, or one that is clearly worse lying down or wakes you with vomiting. A brand-new, severe orthostatic headache also deserves prompt evaluation. When in doubt, be seen.

## The bottom line

Headaches in POTS, dysautonomia and long COVID are not a sign that you are drinking too little water or imagining things. They are mostly the visible edge of a circulation that cannot keep your head steadily supplied when you stand, layered on a genuine overlap with migraine. That reframing is useful because it points at real levers. Ask what posture does to your headache: if it eases when you lie down, the orthostatic toolkit of fluids, salt, compression and pacing is where to start, and if it throbs regardless of position, that is migraine and needs its own treatment. Track the pattern against your HRV and a stand test so you can tell what is working. And keep the short red-flag list handy, because a sudden, severe, or position-flipped headache is the one time this symptom is worth moving quickly on.
