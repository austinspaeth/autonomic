You are trying to be careful with your body, and marijuana sits in an awkward spot. It can ease nausea or quiet a racing mind at night, but you have also noticed the next morning feels harder, your heart runs faster, and standing up is rougher than usual. So the plain question is worth a plain answer: **does marijuana make POTS worse?** For most people with POTS, the core cardiovascular effects of cannabis push in exactly the wrong direction, because THC speeds up your heart and can lower your blood pressure when you stand, which are the two problems [POTS](/insights/pots/pots-subtypes-explained/) is defined by. That does not mean it harms everyone the same way, and some people use it for symptoms and feel better overall, so the rest of this is about the physiology, the honest limits of the evidence, and how to find your own answer.

These are educational field notes, not medical advice. Nothing here diagnoses or treats anything, cannabis laws and products vary widely, and if you take heart-rate or blood-pressure medication, or you use cannabis regularly, that is a conversation for your clinician.

## What marijuana does to your heart rate

The most reliable effect of THC, the main psychoactive compound in marijuana, is a faster heart rate. A systematic review and meta-analysis of its effects in humans found that acute doses raised heart rate by about 8 beats per minute on average, and the rise is dose dependent, so stronger products and bigger hits push it higher ([Sultan et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5874709/)). The American Heart Association's scientific statement on cannabis and the heart describes the mechanism plainly: THC stimulates the sympathetic (fight-or-flight) nervous system and inhibits the parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) side, which increases heart rate and supine blood pressure ([Page et al., 2020](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000883)).

That autonomic shift shows up directly in your [heart rate variability](/insights/hrv/hrv-complete-guide/). In a study measuring HRV before and immediately after people smoked cannabis, the parasympathetic markers (HF power and [RMSSD](/insights/basics/rmssd-and-pnn50-vagal-tone-metrics/)) fell significantly while heart rate rose by about 7 beats per minute ([Keen et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40628347/)). In other words, cannabis nudges you toward the stressed, low-variability state, which is the opposite of the calm you may be chasing and the opposite of what a recovering nervous system needs.

For someone without POTS, an extra 8 beats per minute is minor. For someone whose heart already jumps 30 or more beats on standing, there is much less margin, so the same bump can mean the difference between a manageable morning and a lightheaded, pounding one.

## What it does when you stand up

The bigger problem for POTS is what cannabis does to blood pressure during posture changes. Cannabinoids relax blood vessels, and when you stand, that works against the narrowing your body relies on to push blood back up from your legs. A meta-analysis of 46 randomized trials of medical cannabinoids found that their use was associated with a roughly threefold higher risk of orthostatic hypotension, a drop in blood pressure on standing (relative risk 3.16), and a higher risk of low blood pressure overall ([Watanabe et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.japh.2021.03.013)).

POTS is partly a problem of blood pooling in the lower body and not enough volume returning to the heart when you stand, so anything that widens your vessels makes that pooling worse. That is the same reason [alcohol hits POTS hard](/insights/hrv/alcohol-and-hrv-pots/), and it is why a stand test the morning after use often looks worse: a bigger heart-rate rise, more symptoms, and sometimes a visible dip in standing blood pressure. If you want to see this for yourself, the [orthostatic stand test](/insights/pots/the-orthostatic-stand-test-at-home/) is the single most useful measurement to compare on use and non-use days.

## THC and CBD are not the same thing

Cannabis is not one drug, and the two best-known compounds behave differently, so it is worth separating them.

| | THC | CBD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intoxicating ("high") | Yes | No |
| Effect on heart rate | Raises it, dose dependent | Usually small; one study found a mild rise |
| Effect on blood pressure | Raises supine, can drop on standing | Lowers resting blood pressure |
| Autonomic shift | Toward sympathetic (stress) | Calming, but still lowers blood pressure |
| Main POTS concern | Faster heart, orthostatic drop | Low blood pressure, drug interactions |

**THC** is the compound behind the racing heart and the orthostatic drop above. **CBD** does not get you high and is far gentler, which is why many people with POTS reach for it instead. But gentler is not the same as neutral. In a randomized study, a single 600 mg dose of CBD lowered resting systolic blood pressure by about 6 mmHg in healthy volunteers ([Jadoon et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1172/jci.insight.93760)), and low blood pressure is exactly what drives standing symptoms in much of POTS. The AHA statement notes that CBD tends to reduce heart rate and blood pressure overall ([Page et al., 2020](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000883)). So CBD avoids the tachycardia problem but can feed the low-blood-pressure one.

<div class="callout callout-warn"><strong>CBD and your medications need care.</strong> CBD changes how your liver processes many drugs, which can raise or lower their levels in your body. If you take a beta blocker, ivabradine, midodrine, fludrocortisone or anything else for heart rate or blood pressure, talk to your clinician or pharmacist before adding CBD rather than experimenting, and never use cannabis to "calm" palpitations.</div>

## But some people say it helps, and that can be true

None of this is a verdict on everyone. People with POTS, long COVID and dysautonomia often live with nausea, pain, anxiety and broken sleep, and some find that cannabis genuinely eases those, which matters to quality of life. Two honest points sit on either side of that.

First, there are no clinical trials of cannabis as a treatment for POTS, in either direction. Reviews of its cardiovascular effects consistently report a faster heart rate and episodes of low blood pressure on standing ([Watanabe et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.japh.2021.03.013)), but nobody has run a proper study in POTS patients, so anyone promising it treats POTS is guessing.

Second, a symptom improving does not mean your physiology improved. The same dose that settles your [nausea](/insights/pots/nausea-in-pots-dysautonomia/) tonight is also speeding your heart and widening your vessels, so you can feel better and stand worse at the same time. This matters most for the [hyperadrenergic subtype](/insights/pots/pots-subtypes-explained/), where the sympathetic nervous system is already overactive and THC adds more of the same signal. It is a reasonable thing to treat with extra caution and to raise with your specialist.

<div class="callout callout-note"><strong>The hot-shower clue is worth knowing.</strong> Heavy, long-term cannabis use can cause cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome, marked by cycles of severe nausea and vomiting, and relief from hot showers or baths (<a href="https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2824833">Angulo, 2024</a>). That hot-water relief is easy to mistake for ordinary POTS behavior, even though for POTS a <a href="/insights/pots/hot-showers-pots/">hot shower usually makes things worse</a>. If your nausea got worse the more you used, and only a scalding shower helps, that pattern is worth showing a doctor.</div>

## Chronic use, tolerance and why experiences differ

If cannabis is so unhelpful for the heart, why do some long-term users report feeling fine, or even calmer? Part of the answer is tolerance: with repeated use the body adapts to some of the cardiovascular effects, so the heart-rate spike that floors a new user can soften in someone who uses daily. The research on heart rate variability in regular users is genuinely mixed, with studies pointing in different directions, so there is no clean chronic signal to lean on. That uncertainty is the point. Your dose, your product, your subtype and your tolerance all shift the result, which is why a population average cannot tell you your own answer, and your own measurements can.

## How to test your own response

The honest way through all of this is the same self-tracking loop that works for any trigger: hold everything else steady and let the data show you the pattern, without judgment.

| Step | What to do | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Log it | Record each use as a trigger, with rough amount and type | The honest count matters more than memory |
| Measure the same way | Same posture and time every morning, first thing | Consistency lets a real effect show through noise |
| Watch three numbers | Morning HRV, resting heart rate, stand test | Cannabis usually moves all three the same direction |
| Compare weeks | A use week against a non-use week | One rough morning proves nothing; a week does |

See [how to find your triggers with a symptom journal](/insights/recovery/find-your-triggers-symptom-journal/) for the general method, and compare your experience with [caffeine's effect on POTS](/insights/pots/caffeine-and-pots-heart-rate-hrv/), the other everyday substance people most want to pin down. Do this for a week or two and you stop guessing. You will know whether cannabis sits inside your normal noise or clearly worsens your standing, and that is worth far more than any general rule.

## The bottom line

Does marijuana make POTS worse? For most people, its core effects work against the diagnosis: THC speeds your heart by roughly 8 beats per minute, shifts your nervous system toward stress so your HRV falls, and cannabinoids are linked to a higher chance of blood pressure dropping when you stand. CBD is gentler and does not cause the racing heart, but it also lowers blood pressure, which is the wrong direction for orthostatic symptoms. There are no trials of cannabis as a POTS treatment, and some people still use it for nausea, pain or sleep and feel better overall, so this is a personal trade-off rather than a universal rule. The good news is that it is one of the easier things to test: log your use, measure the same way each morning, and let a week of data show you your own line.

<div class="callout callout-warn"><strong>Not medical advice.</strong> This article is educational and meant to help you understand and track your own data, not to diagnose or treat any condition. Cannabis laws, products and potencies vary widely, and it can interact with medications and other conditions. If you are concerned about your symptoms, your readings, or your cannabis use, or you take medication that could interact with it, work with a clinician who can advise you properly. Never start, stop or change a medication based on an article.</div>
