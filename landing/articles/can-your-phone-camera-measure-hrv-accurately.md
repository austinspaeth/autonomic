If you want to start tracking your HRV but don't want to buy a watch or a chest strap first, your phone's camera can take a reading. The fair question is whether it is good enough to be worth doing. **At rest, the studies say yes: fingertip camera readings of RMSSD agree closely with an ECG, closely enough to follow your own day-to-day trend. They get much less accurate with movement, right after exercise and if you press too hard, so how you take the reading matters as much as the phone.** Here is what the research shows and how to get a reading you can trust.

## How does a phone camera measure HRV?

When you rest a fingertip over the rear camera with the flash on, the light shines into your skin. Each time your heart beats, a small surge of blood reaches your fingertip and absorbs a little more light, so the camera sees your finger darken and lighten very slightly with every pulse. This is called photoplethysmography, or PPG, and it is the same idea most smartwatches and rings use, just with your camera and flash instead of a built-in sensor.

The app times the gap between each pulse, then works out how much those gaps vary. That variation is your heart rate variability. Most apps, Autonomic included, report RMSSD, a measure of the quick beat-to-beat changes driven by the calming, parasympathetic side of your nervous system. If you are not sure which HRV number you are looking at, [RMSSD vs SDNN](/insights/hrv/rmssd-vs-sdnn/) explains the difference.

## How accurate is a phone camera compared with an ECG?

An ECG, which records the heart's electrical signal, is the gold standard for HRV. Several studies have recorded camera readings and an ECG at the same time.

The most cited compared a smartphone camera, a Polar chest strap and an ECG in 29 healthy adults during 5 minutes of rest ([Plews et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2016-0668)). Both the camera and the strap agreed with the ECG on RMSSD, with differences rated "trivial" and a typical error of about 6%. The authors suggested camera readings may be a preferred choice for athletes simply because they are so practical.

Others found much the same at rest. Using an iPhone 6 recording at 240 frames a second, one team found the camera's RMSSD differed from the ECG by 1.79 ms on average, not far from the 1.21 ms difference between two leads of the same ECG ([Bánhalmi et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1155/2018/4038034)). In 30 healthy adults, 14 of 16 HRV measures from a phone camera correlated strongly with the ECG, though only 7 of them agreed closely enough to be swapped in for it ([Peng et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1155/2015/516826)). Plain heart rate is easier still: in 40 healthy adults, fingertip camera heart rate at rest was off from the ECG by about 1 beat per minute on average ([Yan et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.2196/mhealth.7275)).

These studies were small and mostly in healthy young adults, so they show what a camera can do in good conditions. They are not a guarantee for every phone, every app or every body.

## Where do camera readings fall short?

The same studies that look good at rest show where the method struggles. In 31 adults measured before and after weight training, camera readings were comparable to an ECG at rest, but the gap grew much larger right after exercise ([Holmes et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/s20205738)). A review of studies comparing pulse-based readings with an ECG reached the same conclusion: accuracy is good at rest, but physical activity and some mental stressors can weaken it to an unacceptable degree ([Schäfer and Vagedes, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijcard.2012.03.119)).

There is also a deeper caveat. A camera does not measure your heartbeat directly. It measures your pulse in your finger, so what it really reports is pulse rate variability. Some researchers argue that this is a related but different measure, because the pulse also behaves differently with posture, exercise and where the sensor sits ([Yuda et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40101-020-00233-x)). In a study of 931 adults with chronic conditions, a pulse sensor worn on the upper arm read RMSSD and SDNN lower than an ECG ([Kantrowitz et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2025.1630032)). That study did not test phones, and several of its authors work for the company that makes the sensor, but it is a reminder not to compare camera numbers with ECG or watch numbers. For a wider look at rings, watches and straps, see [how accurate HRV wearables are](/insights/research/how-accurate-are-hrv-wearables/).

<div class="callout callout-note"><strong>Compare like with like.</strong> A camera reading is good for tracking your own trend over weeks. It is not good for comparing your number with a friend's watch or a published "normal" range. Your own baseline, measured the same way each day, is what matters.</div>

## What ruins a camera reading?

Most bad camera readings come down to a handful of things you can control.

- **Pressing too hard.** The camera reads the pulse of blood in your fingertip. Pressing down squeezes that blood out and flattens the very signal it needs. Rest your finger lightly so it covers the lens and flash.
- **Moving.** Shifting your finger, talking or fidgeting adds noise that can look like extra variation. Brace your hand on something and stay still.
- **Cold fingers.** Less blood flowing to a cold fingertip means a weaker pulse for the camera to see. Warm your hands first if they are cold.
- **Light leaking in.** If your fingertip doesn't fully cover the lens, outside light mixes into the signal. Cover the lens and flash completely.
- **Taking it at the wrong time.** Right after exercise, coffee, a shower or a stressful call, your HRV really is different, and the camera's accuracy drops too. A reading taken right after standing up will also look very different from one taken lying down, especially with POTS.

## How do you take a good camera reading?

Consistency does more for your data than any device upgrade. A good routine looks like this.

1. **Pick the same time every day.** First thing in the morning, before you get up, coffee or your phone, is the easiest to repeat. [The best time of day to measure HRV](/insights/hrv/best-time-of-day-to-measure-hrv/) explains why the morning reading wins.
2. **Use the same position.** Lying down or seated, but the same one each time. If you have POTS, measure before you stand up for the day.
3. **Rest your fingertip lightly** over the rear camera and flash, with your hand supported so it doesn't wobble.
4. **Breathe normally and stay still** for the whole reading. Five minutes is the length used in the Plews study and in most HRV research; [how long to measure HRV](/insights/hrv/how-long-to-measure-hrv-reading-length/) covers shorter options.
5. **Judge the result against your own baseline,** not against a chart or someone else's number. One reading means little. A week of readings taken the same way shows a pattern.

## When is a chest strap worth it?

For a morning reading at rest, a camera is a perfectly good place to start, and it costs nothing. A chest strap is worth considering if you want readings while you are up and moving, during a stand test or exercise, or overnight, since those are exactly the conditions where camera readings lose accuracy. It also helps if your camera readings keep coming out erratic even when you follow the routine above, or if holding still for five minutes with your finger on a phone is hard on a bad day. If you get to that point, [our chest strap comparison](/insights/hrv/best-hrv-chest-strap-polar-h10-coospo-h808s/) covers two of the most popular options.

## The bottom line

A phone camera can measure HRV accurately enough at rest to track your own trend. In studies comparing fingertip readings with an ECG, resting RMSSD agreed closely, with differences rated trivial to small. Accuracy falls with movement, right after exercise and with a heavy finger, and pulse-based readings are not identical to ECG HRV, so compare camera readings only with your own earlier camera readings. Take it at the same time each morning, in the same position, with a light touch, and the trend will tell you far more than any single number.
