## Sjögren's is more than dry eyes

Sjögren's (pronounced SHOW-grins) is a **systemic autoimmune disease**: the immune system turns on the body's own tissue, and its favorite target is the glands that make tears and saliva. That is why its hallmark is dryness, gritty, burning eyes and a mouth so dry that crackers are hard to swallow ([Brito-Zerón et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrdp.2016.47)). It is increasingly called **Sjögren's disease** rather than syndrome, to reflect that it reaches far beyond the eyes and mouth.

Because it is systemic, Sjögren's can also bring joint pain, rashes, lung and kidney involvement, and a deep, persistent fatigue. Abnormal fatigue was present in **67 percent** of people with primary Sjögren's in one study, and most of them were not depressed ([Segal et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1002/art.24311)).

It is diagnosed overwhelmingly in women, about ten for every man, usually in middle age, and a large meta-analysis put its prevalence at roughly 61 people per 100,000 ([Qin et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1136/annrheumdis-2014-205375)). It is also thought to be underdiagnosed, partly because dryness is easy to blame on age, screens, medications or the weather.

## Where it meets the autonomic nervous system

Tears and saliva are not made on their own. The autonomic nervous system tells those glands when to work, the same system that sets heart rate, squeezes blood vessels on standing and moves food through the gut. Sjögren's can affect that system in several ways, and the research is consistent that it does.

**Autonomic symptoms are common.** When 38 people with primary Sjögren's completed a validated autonomic symptom questionnaire, they scored higher than 200 population controls on both sides of the system: the parasympathetic side (secretions, bladder, pupils and, in women, gastroparesis) and the sympathetic side (orthostatic intolerance and blood vessel control). **45 percent** had a total score at least two standard deviations above normal, and objective autonomic tests showed dysfunction too ([Mandl et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/rheumatology/ken107)).

**Dizziness on standing can be the leading problem.** In a Mayo Clinic series of 13 people whose autonomic impairment was traced to Sjögren's, **every one** had lightheadedness on standing and fainting or near-fainting. Tilt testing found orthostatic hypotension in 5 and an excessive postural tachycardia or blood pressure rise in 8, and nearly all had gut or bladder symptoms. The authors' conclusion was direct: Sjögren's should be actively looked for in people with autonomic symptoms ([Goodman et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1097/NRL.0000000000000134)).

**Small fiber neuropathy is a major route in.** Sjögren's is one of the main recognized causes of small fiber neuropathy, the damage to thin nerves that carry pain, temperature and autonomic signals ([Sène, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbspin.2017.11.002)). In a study of people with Sjögren's and biopsy-proven small fiber neuropathy, about **45 percent had nerve pain before any dryness**, and they were less likely to carry the anti-Ro antibodies that most Sjögren's workups lean on ([Birnbaum et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/acr.23762)). Our [small fiber neuropathy guide](/insights/pots/small-fiber-neuropathy-pots-long-covid/) explains how that kind of nerve damage leads to blood pooling and a racing heart.

## The overlap runs both ways

Here is the part that trips people up. If you already have [POTS](/conditions/pots/) or [dysautonomia](/conditions/dysautonomia/), dry eyes and a dry mouth do not automatically mean Sjögren's. The autonomic nerves drive tear and saliva production, so dysautonomia can dry them on its own, and so can several medications. Our guide to [blurry vision and visual symptoms in POTS](/insights/pots/blurry-vision-visual-symptoms-pots-dysautonomia/) covers the eye side of this.

| Dryness from | What else tends to come with it | Who usually sorts it out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sjögren's | Joint pain, deep fatigue, swollen salivary glands, positive anti-SSA/Ro or lip biopsy | Rheumatologist |
| Dysautonomia | Racing heart or dizziness on standing, sweating changes, gut and bladder symptoms | Autonomic specialist, cardiologist or neurologist |
| Medications | Starts or worsens after a new drug or dose (antihistamines, some antidepressants, some blood pressure drugs) | Your prescriber |

The reverse matters just as much. Autoimmunity is more common in POTS than in the general population: in a review of 100 consecutive POTS patients, 25 percent had positive antinuclear antibodies and 20 percent had a diagnosed autoimmune disorder, about double the highest general population estimate ([Blitshteyn, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0961203315587566)). The most common was Hashimoto's thyroiditis, which our [thyroid and HRV guide](/insights/pots/thyroid-hrv-pots/) covers. The point is not that every POTS case is autoimmune. It is that an autoimmune driver is worth ruling in or out, because it can change treatment.

## How Sjögren's is diagnosed

There is no single test. Clinicians use the 2016 ACR/EULAR classification criteria, which add up five weighted findings in someone with suggestive symptoms. A total of **4 or more** meets the criteria ([Shiboski et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/art.39859)).

That last point matters for people with autonomic symptoms. The research above found that the people whose Sjögren's showed up first as nerve pain were *less* likely to have the antibodies. A negative blood test alone does not close the question, and a rheumatologist may suggest a lip biopsy if the picture fits.

If autonomic symptoms are part of your story, the workup often widens to include a [stand test or tilt table test](/insights/pots/tilt-table-test-what-to-expect/), [autonomic function testing](/insights/pots/autonomic-function-tests-what-to-expect/) and, for burning or tingling, a skin biopsy for small fiber neuropathy.

## Questions worth bringing to your appointment

- I have dizziness or a racing heart on standing, and dry eyes or a dry mouth that will not go away. Could Sjögren's be part of this?
- My anti-SSA/Ro test was negative. Would a lip biopsy, Schirmer test or saliva flow test still be useful?
- I have burning or tingling in my feet or hands. Should I be tested for small fiber neuropathy?
- Could any of my medications be adding to the dryness?
- If this is autoimmune, would that change how my autonomic symptoms are treated?

Bringing a record helps those questions land. A few weeks of standing heart rate, morning readings, fatigue and dryness logged by date gives a rheumatologist and an autonomic specialist the same picture, rather than two partial ones. Our guide to [turning your data into a doctor conversation](/insights/recovery/turn-your-data-into-a-doctor-conversation/) shows how to keep it to one page.

## What to track at home

You cannot test for Sjögren's at home. What you can do is record the autonomic side of it, and the symptoms that are easy to forget by the time you see someone.

- **Standing heart rate.** A [home stand test](/insights/pots/the-orthostatic-stand-test-at-home/) puts a number on what standing does to you. A sustained rise of 30 beats per minute or more (40 in teens) is the POTS pattern; a fall in blood pressure points toward [orthostatic hypotension](/insights/pots/orthostatic-hypotension-vs-pots/).
- **A morning HRV reading**, measured the same way each day, so changes during a flare or after a treatment change show up against your own baseline. The [HRV baseline guide](/insights/hrv/hrv-baseline-how-to-build-and-use-it/) explains why your own normal matters.
- **Fatigue and effort**, so you can see whether crashes follow busy days, poor sleep or standing a lot. [Pacing 101](/insights/recovery/pacing-101-energy-envelope/) helps when fatigue sets the limits.
- **Dryness, pain and flares** as they happen, with what you were doing: heat, screens, a new medication, a stressful week. The [symptom journal guide](/insights/recovery/find-your-triggers-symptom-journal/) keeps it to a couple of taps.
- **Fluids.** If you have POTS features, how much you drink matters for your standing symptoms, and dry mouth can change how much you drink. See [salt and fluids for POTS](/insights/research/science-of-salt-and-fluids-for-pots/).

## The bottom line

Sjögren's is a whole-body autoimmune disease best known for dry eyes and a dry mouth, and it can reach the autonomic nerves. Autonomic symptoms, including dizziness on standing, are common in it, it is one of the main causes of small fiber neuropathy, and its nerve symptoms can arrive before the dryness and without the usual antibodies. The overlap runs both ways, because dysautonomia can cause dryness on its own. If you have POTS or dysautonomia and persistent dryness, it is a fair question to ask, and a record of your standing heart rate, morning readings, fatigue and symptoms helps your clinicians answer it.

Sjögren's, POTS and related conditions need proper evaluation, usually by a rheumatologist and an autonomic specialist. If your symptoms are new, worsening or frightening, get medical care.
