## The moment your heart rate spikes

If you have POTS, you know the feeling. You stand up to do something ordinary, and within a few seconds your heart is pounding at 130, 140, maybe 150, your head goes light, and your whole system feels like it has been yanked into high alert. The question in that moment is not about long-term management. It is: how do I lower this high heart rate from POTS right now?

The good news is that the physiology points to a short, reliable list of things that work in seconds to a couple of minutes, none of which need a prescription. The better news is that the spike is almost always your body compensating for standing, not a dangerous rhythm. This article is educational field notes, not medical advice, but it gives you an order to reach for and a way to tell whether each move actually helps you.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <strong>The one-sentence version.</strong> Get your blood back toward your heart (lie down or raise your legs, tense your lower-body muscles), slow your breathing with a long exhale, and get cold fluid and salt in, all at once. The racing heart is defending your blood pressure, so give it less to defend against.
</div>

## Why your heart races when you stand

To stand is to fight gravity. The instant you are upright, somewhere between 300 and 800 ml of blood wants to settle into the veins of your legs and, especially, your abdomen. In a healthy system, reflexes squeeze those vessels down and blood keeps circulating. In POTS, that clamp-down is incomplete, so blood pools, less returns to your heart, and the amount pumped per beat drops.

Your body will not let blood pressure fall if it can help it, so it does the one thing it can do fast: it raises your heart rate, hard. That is the standing spike. A sustained rise of **30 beats per minute or more** within ten minutes of standing (**40 or more** in teenagers) is the defining feature of POTS. So a jump to 150 is not a second, separate heart problem layered on top. It is the same mechanism doing exactly what it does. We go deeper into the pooling itself in [blood pooling in POTS legs](/insights/pots/blood-pooling-in-pots-legs/), and into how to measure the rise in [the orthostatic stand test at home](/insights/pots/the-orthostatic-stand-test-at-home/).

Understanding this changes the whole emotional tone of the moment. The heart rate is a symptom of too little blood coming back, not the heart failing. Every move below works by fixing that underlying problem, which is why they calm the rate instead of just masking it.

## The in-the-moment toolkit, in order

Reach for these roughly top to bottom, but there is no harm in doing several at once. Speed is the point, so start before the symptom peaks rather than waiting to see how bad it gets.

### 1. Change your position

This is the single most powerful lever, because it removes the thing causing the spike. If you can lie down, do, and prop your legs up on a wall, a couch arm, or a cushion so they are above your heart. Within seconds the pooled blood drains back and your heart rate starts falling. If you cannot lie down, sit and put your feet up on a chair, or lean forward with your head low and your chest toward your knees. Even crossing your legs tightly while seated helps. Squatting fully, if you can get there safely, is one of the fastest position changes of all.

### 2. Counterpressure: turn your muscles into a pump

Your leg and abdominal muscles wrap around your deep veins. Contract them hard and you squeeze pooled blood back toward your heart, which refills it and raises blood pressure within seconds, so your heart does not need to race as much. Cross your legs and tense everything, clench your buttocks and abdomen, or grip your hands and tense your arms if you are seated and cannot move your legs. These **counterpressure maneuvers** have real trial support for fainting and apply directly to the POTS spike. Full technique guide: [counterpressure maneuvers for POTS](/insights/pots/counterpressure-maneuvers-pots-orthostatic-intolerance/).

### 3. Slow your breathing, with a long exhale

Your heart rate naturally rises a little as you breathe in and falls as you breathe out. You can lean on that. Slow your breathing to about **six breaths a minute**: in through the nose for roughly four seconds, out gently for about six. Keep the exhale longer than the inhale, because the exhale is where the vagal brake pulls your rate down. Do not force a big breath or hold it, which can backfire. Five or six slow breaths is often enough to take the edge off. This is the same mechanism behind [resonant breathing and HRV biofeedback](/insights/hrv/resonant-breathing-hrv-biofeedback/), which trains the response so it is there when you need it.

### 4. Cold and fluid

Cold is a quick parasympathetic nudge: sip ice water, hold something cold to your face or the back of your neck, or splash cool water on your face. At the same time, drink. A fast glass or two of water, ideally with salt or an electrolyte mix, pulls fluid into your bloodstream and raises the volume your heart has to work with. Many people with POTS use a rapid "water bolus" of roughly 300 to 500 ml for exactly this acute effect. More on why this works in [the science of salt and fluids for POTS](/insights/research/science-of-salt-and-fluids-for-pots/).

### 5. Remove the trigger

Sometimes the spike has an obvious cause stacked on top of standing: a hot room, a hot shower, a big meal, a long stretch on your feet, or a wave of anxiety. Step out of the heat, sit down after eating, and let the immediate stressor pass. Heat in particular dilates your vessels and makes pooling worse, so cooling down does double duty. See [heat intolerance and POTS](/insights/pots/heat-intolerance-and-pots/).

## A reference table for the moment

| Move | How fast | Why it works | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lie down, legs up | Seconds | Removes the gravity load; pooled blood drains back to the heart | None; it is the safest first move |
| Counterpressure (tense legs, clench) | Seconds | Muscle pump pushes pooled blood centrally, raises blood pressure | Do it early, before symptoms peak |
| Cold water or cold to the face | Seconds | A quick parasympathetic (vagal) nudge | Very intense cold can feel jarring |
| Slow breathing, long exhale | 30 to 60 seconds | Lengthened exhale pulls the vagal brake on heart rate | Do not force or hold the breath |
| Rapid fluid, with salt | 2 to 20 minutes | Expands blood volume so the heart has more to pump | Discuss daily salt load with your clinician |
| Leave the heat or sit after eating | Minutes | Removes a trigger stacked on top of standing | Plan ahead for known triggers |

## Is a high heart rate from POTS dangerous?

For most people with POTS, the honest answer is reassuring: the standing spike is a compensation, the heart is structurally healthy, and rates of 120 to 150 on standing are uncomfortable rather than harmful. The racing feeling and the fear it produces are real, and the fear itself raises your heart rate further, which is part of why calm, slow breathing helps on two fronts at once. If a racing heart and a sense of dread tend to travel together for you, [is it POTS or anxiety](/insights/pots/is-it-pots-or-anxiety/) untangles the overlap, and [palpitations and ectopic beats](/insights/pots/palpitations-ectopic-beats-pots-hrv/) covers the fluttery, skipped-beat sensation specifically.

What is worth taking seriously, and bringing to a clinician promptly, is a different pattern:

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <strong>See a clinician about any of these.</strong> New or severe chest pain or pressure; actually fainting (losing consciousness), not just feeling you might; a fast heart rate that does <em>not</em> settle when you are lying down and resting; a pulse that feels irregular or chaotic rather than just fast; or a sudden, large change from your usual pattern. These can point to something other than, or on top of, POTS. This article is general education and cannot diagnose you.
</div>

It also helps to know that a persistently high heart rate even at rest, without the standing trigger, points more toward [inappropriate sinus tachycardia](/insights/pots/inappropriate-sinus-tachycardia-vs-pots/) than classic POTS, which is a distinction a clinician can sort out and which sometimes changes treatment.

## The longer game: fewer spikes, smaller spikes

In-the-moment moves are the fire extinguisher. The way to need them less often is to attack the pooling and low blood volume over weeks: steady fluids and salt, compression garments on the legs and abdomen, and gradual, mostly-recumbent reconditioning so your muscle pump and blood volume rebuild. For some people a clinician will add a low-dose medication, commonly a beta-blocker or [ivabradine](/insights/pots/ivabradine-for-pots/), to blunt the rise directly. Those are decisions to make with a professional, on top of the basics rather than instead of them.

The piece people most often miss is feedback. It is genuinely hard to tell whether compression, or a new fluid routine, or a breathing practice is working, because any single day is noisy and a spike feels the same whether your baseline is improving or not. That is where measuring beats guessing.

## FAQ

**How do I lower a high heart rate from POTS quickly?** Change position first: lie down and raise your legs, or sit with your feet up. At the same time, cross and tense your legs, clench your buttocks and abdomen, slow your breathing to about six breaths a minute with a longer exhale, and sip cold fluid with salt. Most spikes ease within a minute or two once blood is returning to your heart.

**Is a high heart rate from POTS dangerous?** Usually it is a normal compensation for standing, not a dangerous arrhythmia, and the heart is structurally healthy in most people with POTS. Rates of 120 to 150 standing are common. But new chest pain, true fainting, or a fast rate that will not settle at rest should be checked by a clinician.

**What is the best breathing to slow your heart rate?** Slow, even breathing around five to seven breaths a minute, exhale longer than the inhale, for example in for four seconds and out for six. The long exhale is the part that pulls your heart rate down.

**Why does my heart rate spike to 150 when I stand?** Blood pools in your legs and abdomen on standing, less returns to your heart, and your body raises heart rate sharply to defend blood pressure. A sustained rise of 30 bpm or more (40 in teens) is the defining POTS pattern, so a jump to 150 is that mechanism, not a separate heart problem.

**Do beta-blockers or medication fix the spike?** Low-dose beta-blockers and ivabradine can blunt the rise for some people and are prescribed for it, but that is a clinician's decision and works best alongside fluids, salt, compression and reconditioning.
