## Does melatonin actually help with sleep and HRV?

Melatonin is one of the most common supplements in POTS, dysautonomia and long COVID communities, usually reached for because nights are a mess: lying awake wired but exhausted, a clock that drifts later and later, or sleep that never feels deep enough to pay off the next morning. So it is fair to ask the direct question. Does **melatonin** help your sleep, and does it do anything for your HRV? The honest answer is yes, often, but almost never in the way the bottle implies, and the gap between how melatonin works and how most people use it is the whole point of this article.

The single most useful thing to understand is this: melatonin is not a sedative. It is a hormone your brain releases in darkness to tell the rest of your body that it is night. Its most reliable job is **timing**, not knockout power. That reframes the dose, the schedule and your expectations all at once, and it explains why a small amount taken at a consistent time usually outperforms a big dose swallowed late.

## What melatonin actually is

Your pineal gland makes melatonin on a schedule set by light. During the day, bright light keeps melatonin low. As evening comes and light dims, melatonin starts to rise a couple of hours before your usual sleep time, a moment researchers call the dim-light melatonin onset. It peaks in the middle of the night and falls toward morning, clearing the way for cortisol and the start of the day. That rhythm is the body's internal clock written in a single molecule.

Natural nighttime levels are small, measured in picograms per milliliter of blood. This is worth sitting with, because the 10 mg tablet on the shelf pushes blood melatonin far above anything your body would ever produce. Flooding the system does not make the night signal "louder" in a useful way; past a point it mostly adds side effects and a groggy morning. The signal is about **presence and timing**, not volume.

## Melatonin and HRV: what the evidence shows

Here is the careful version, because this is where it is easy to overpromise. The night, under healthy conditions, is when your parasympathetic ("rest and digest") system takes over and HRV rises. Melatonin travels with that shift, and some laboratory studies report that melatonin is associated with higher nighttime vagal activity and that a dose can modestly raise certain HRV measures in the hours after taking it. The mechanism is plausible, and the direction is encouraging.

But the direct, reliable "melatonin raises your HRV" claim is weaker than supplement marketing suggests, and the studies are small and mixed. The dependable route is the same one that runs through every good sleep habit: **better sleep lifts HRV**. If melatonin helps you fall asleep closer to the right time, spend more of the night actually asleep, and wake less, your overnight heart rate settles lower and your morning [RMSSD](/insights/basics/rmssd-and-pnn50-vagal-tone-metrics/) tends to rise. That is a downstream effect of a better night, not melatonin acting on your heart like a drug. For how sleep and autonomic recovery connect, see [sleep and autonomic recovery](/insights/recovery/sleep-and-autonomic-recovery/), and for what a good night looks like in the data, [overnight heart rate while you sleep](/insights/basics/overnight-heart-rate-while-you-sleep/).

So the honest framing is: melatonin may help your HRV, mostly by helping your sleep, and the only way to know whether it does that for you is to watch your own trend. A number that drifts up over a couple of weeks of better nights means more than any single morning.

## Melatonin, blood pressure and the POTS caution

This is the part supplement pages tend to skip, and it is the one most relevant if you have POTS or orthostatic intolerance. Melatonin has a mild **blood-pressure-lowering** effect at night. It is studied as a gentle support for the normal overnight blood-pressure **dip**, the healthy drop of roughly 10 to 20 percent during sleep. People whose pressure fails to dip at night (a pattern covered in [non-dipping blood pressure](/insights/basics/non-dipping-blood-pressure-nocturnal-dip/)) are sometimes exactly the people melatonin's nighttime effect is studied in.

For most people this is harmless, and plenty with POTS take melatonin comfortably and sleep better for it. But if you already run **low or borderline blood pressure**, or you take a medication that lowers it, melatonin's overnight effect can add to that. The practical consequences are small and manageable, but worth naming: take it at night when you are lying down rather than during upright hours, start low, and pay attention to how you feel standing up the next morning, since mornings are already the hardest part of the day for many with POTS (see [why POTS symptoms are worse in the morning](/insights/pots/pots-symptoms-worse-in-the-morning/)). A simple way to keep an eye on it is the at-home stand test; if your standing heart-rate rise climbs or you feel more lightheaded after starting melatonin, note it and raise it with your clinician.

## Why timing beats dose

Because melatonin is a clock signal, **when** you take it can matter as much as how much. Two different problems call for two different approaches.

If the problem is simply falling asleep at a reasonable time, a low dose 30 to 60 minutes before your target sleep reinforces the natural evening rise. If the problem is a **delayed body clock**, the "wired but tired" pattern where you cannot fall asleep until the small hours (common in POTS and long COVID, and discussed in [POTS insomnia](/insights/pots/pots-insomnia-wired-but-tired/)), then a small dose taken earlier in the evening can gradually shift your clock earlier over several days. That is a timing intervention, not a sedative one, and the amount is almost beside the point.

## How much melatonin, and the label problem

Less than most bottles suggest. Because natural levels are tiny, physiological doses of about **0.3 to 0.5 mg** are often as effective for sleep onset as the 5 or 10 mg products that dominate the shelf, and they leave less of a groggy morning. Here is a simple reference for matching the approach to the problem.

| Approach | Typical dose | Best for | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Physiological (low) | 0.3 to 0.5 mg | Matching natural levels, gentle sleep-onset help | Often as effective as higher doses, with the least next-day grogginess |
| Common over-the-counter | 1 to 3 mg | Occasional trouble falling asleep | A reasonable starting range. More is not reliably better |
| High over-the-counter | 5 to 10 mg | Widely sold, rarely necessary | Pushes blood levels far above natural; more grogginess and vivid dreams |
| Timing-focused (not dose) | A low dose, 2 to 5 hours before target sleep | Shifting a delayed body clock earlier | The timing carries the effect; get clinician guidance for this use |

There is a second catch that has nothing to do with dose: over-the-counter melatonin is poorly regulated, and the amount in the pill often does not match the label. An independent analysis of commercial melatonin products found actual content ranging from far below to several times the stated amount, with some products containing other compounds entirely. That is a real reason an effect can feel inconsistent from bottle to bottle, and a good argument for a reputable brand and for judging the result by how you sleep, not by the number on the front.

## Side effects, interactions and who should be careful

Melatonin is generally well tolerated in the short term, and the most common complaints are predictable: daytime drowsiness or a groggy morning (usually a sign of too much or too late), vivid dreams, and headache. Taking it in the daytime can shift your clock the wrong way, so keep it to the evening unless a clinician is guiding a specific schedule.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <strong>When to talk to a clinician first.</strong> Melatonin can interact with several medications and conditions, so check before starting if any of these apply to you: blood thinners such as warfarin; blood-pressure medication (the overnight lowering can add up, a particular point in POTS); diabetes medication (melatonin can affect glucose handling); immunosuppressants and autoimmune conditions; seizure disorders; and other sedatives. It is not recommended in pregnancy or breastfeeding, or for children, without medical guidance. If you have ongoing insomnia for months rather than an occasional rough patch, that deserves a proper look rather than an indefinite nightly pill.
</div>

## How to tell if melatonin is working for you

Because the direct HRV effect is modest and the sleep effect is real but not universal, and because the pill itself may not contain what it claims, the only trustworthy answer is your own data. Supplements like this are a good example of a change you can run as a quiet, careful experiment.

The approach is simple. Change one thing at a time, hold the dose and timing steady for two to three weeks, and measure the same way each morning: same posture, same time, same reading length, as in the [measuring-well guide](/insights/basics/how-to-measure-hrv-accurately-at-home/). Then read the **trend**, not any single morning, judged against your own [rolling baseline](/insights/hrv/hrv-baseline-how-to-build-and-use-it/) rather than a number from the internet. What you are looking for is a gentle lift in your morning HRV, a lower overnight resting heart rate, fewer awakenings, and no new lightheadedness when you stand up. If the melatonin review of your own nights shows nothing after a fair trial, that is a real answer too, and a reason to stop rather than keep taking it out of habit.

## The bottom line

Melatonin is a timing signal, not a sedative, and using it that way is most of the battle. For POTS, dysautonomia and long COVID sleep, a low dose at a consistent time usually beats a big dose taken late, bright morning light does at least as much work for a delayed clock, and the clearest benefit to your HRV comes indirectly, through a better night rather than through anything melatonin does to your heart. Mind the mild overnight blood-pressure effect if you run low, pick a reputable brand because the label cannot be trusted at face value, and let your own tracked trend, not a marketing claim, decide whether it earns a place in your routine.
