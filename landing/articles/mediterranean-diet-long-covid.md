If you have long COVID, you have probably been told to "eat anti-inflammatory," usually with a long list of rules and no evidence attached. So does an anti-inflammatory diet for long COVID actually help? The honest answer is that it may help a little, the Mediterranean diet is the version with the most research behind it, and none of that research yet proves it changes the course of the illness. That still makes it a sensible, low-risk foundation, as long as you build it around your energy, your budget and your own food reactions.

## What does anti-inflammatory eating mean?

"Anti-inflammatory diet" is not a medical diet with a fixed definition. In practice it means a pattern built mostly from vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, olive oil, fish and nuts, with little ultra-processed food, refined grain, added sugar or processed meat. The Mediterranean diet is the best-studied version of that idea, which is why nearly all the long COVID diet research uses it.

Why might it matter? Long COVID appears to involve lingering immune activation and inflammation in at least some people, alongside other processes covered in [what causes long COVID](/insights/postviral/what-causes-long-covid/). A way of eating rich in fiber, polyphenols and unsaturated fats is a plausible support for that, though plausible is not proven.

There is direct trial evidence for at least one ingredient. A meta-analysis of 30 randomized trials with 3,106 participants found that olive oil lowered C-reactive protein (CRP) by 0.64 mg/L on average across 15 trials, and lowered interleukin-6 (IL-6) too, compared with control diets ([Schwingshackl et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu7095356)). Those trials were in general populations, not long COVID, and the authors asked for cautious reading because the trials varied a lot.

Here is the pattern at a glance. The left column is the everyday foundation, the right column is occasional.

| Eat more (most days) | Eat less (occasional) |
| --- | --- |
| **Vegetables**: leafy greens, peppers, zucchini, carrots | **Red and processed meat**: bacon, sausage, deli meat |
| **Fruit**: berries, apples, whatever you tolerate | **Refined grains**: white bread, pastries |
| **Legumes**: lentils, chickpeas, beans | **Added sugar**: soda, sweets, sweetened cereal |
| **Whole grains**: oats, barley, brown rice | **Ultra-processed snacks**: chips, packaged baked goods |
| **Extra-virgin olive oil**: your main fat | **Butter and lard** as everyday fats |
| **Fish and seafood**, especially oily fish | **Fried and fast food** |
| **Nuts and seeds**: walnuts, almonds, flax | **Sugary and alcoholic drinks** |

Nothing here is exotic. Most of it is cheap, shelf-stable or frozen, and easy to batch, which matters when fatigue makes cooking hard.

## What does the research show for long COVID?

Three pieces of work carry most of the weight, and each answers a slightly different question.

**Metabolic health.** The BioICOPER study looked at 305 adults with long COVID in Salamanca, Spain (68% women, average age about 53). Those who followed a Mediterranean diet more closely had lower uric acid, lower BMI, smaller waists, higher HDL ("good") cholesterol and fewer components of metabolic syndrome. There was no link with blood sugar ([Suárez-Moreno et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17040656)).

**The gut.** A Madrid study of 188 long COVID patients compared 134 with high Mediterranean diet adherence to 54 with low adherence. The high-adherence group had lower lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, a marker of cell stress and damage), lower D-dimer, glucose, insulin and LDL cholesterol, and a different gut microbiome, with less *Oscillibacter* and more *Akkermansia*. Notably, CRP and IL-6, two standard inflammation markers, did not differ between the groups ([Cuevas-Sierra et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox13111358)).

**The wider picture.** A 2025 scoping review gathered 50 studies on diet and nutrition in long COVID, 17 of them randomized trials. About 76% (38 of 50) reported at least one significant link between a diet or nutrition factor and a long COVID outcome, most often fatigue. But most of those studies tested supplements rather than eating patterns (vitamin D was the most studied, with mixed results), and the review did not grade study quality ([Bigman et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17111802)).

## What are the limits of the evidence?

Be clear-eyed about what these studies can and cannot tell you.

- **No trial yet.** No randomized trial has tested the Mediterranean diet as a treatment for long COVID. The two long COVID studies above are snapshots that compare people who already eat differently.
- **Association, not cause.** People who eat this way may also differ in income, activity, weight or how sick they were to start with. The BioICOPER authors say plainly that their design cannot establish causality.
- **Markers, not symptoms.** BioICOPER measured metabolic markers, not fatigue, brain fog or post-exertional malaise. Better cholesterol is good for you, but it is not the same as feeling better.
- **Small and local.** Both studies come from Spain, with a few hundred people each.
- **Inflammation is not settled.** In the gut study, the classic inflammation markers CRP and IL-6 did not differ, so "it lowers inflammation in long COVID" is still a hypothesis.

What the evidence supports is a modest, low-risk claim: this pattern is good for general metabolic health, and it may support long COVID recovery. That is worth doing. It is not a reason to expect diet to fix a complex post-viral illness, or to delay medical care.

## What to eat with long COVID when you are exhausted

The best anti-inflammatory diet is the one you can keep doing on your worst week. That means few decisions, little chopping and as little standing as possible. Here is one sample day built from cheap, mostly frozen or canned food.

| Meal | Low-effort option | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Breakfast** | Oats cooked in the microwave or soaked overnight, with frozen berries and a spoon of walnuts or ground flax | About 2 minutes, can be made sitting |
| **Lunch** | Rinsed canned chickpeas or white beans, bagged salad, olive oil, salt and pepper, whole-grain bread | No cooking |
| **Snack** | An apple or banana with a handful of nuts, or hummus with baby carrots | No cooking |
| **Dinner** | Sheet pan of frozen fish fillets or chicken thighs with frozen vegetables and olive oil, plus microwave brown rice or a baked potato | One tray in the oven, sit while it cooks |

A few small moves get you most of the way without a kitchen overhaul:

1. **Make extra-virgin olive oil your default fat.** One change, applied everywhere.
2. **Add one plant to a meal you already eat**: a handful of frozen vegetables, a spoon of beans, some berries.
3. **Have fish once a week**, even from the freezer.
4. **Cook double on a better day and freeze single portions** so a bad day needs only a microwave.

If you also have POTS, a whole-food pattern with little processed food can end up lower in salt than you need, so the small, salty meals and fluid advice in [the POTS diet guide](/insights/food/pots-diet-what-to-eat/) sit on top of it. Follow your own clinician's advice on salt.

### A budget grocery list

- **Pantry:** rolled oats, brown rice or whole-wheat pasta, canned chickpeas, beans and lentils, extra-virgin olive oil, dried herbs, garlic, a bag of nuts or seeds.
- **Freezer:** frozen berries, frozen mixed vegetables, frozen fish fillets, chicken thighs.
- **Fresh:** bagged salad, onions, baby carrots, apples, bananas and whatever fruit is cheapest that week.
- **Optional extras:** canned sardines or salmon, plain yogurt, canned tomatoes (all three can bother people who react to histamine; see below).

Frozen and canned versions count. They cost less, keep longer and need no chopping.

## How do you adapt it for histamine reactions?

Here is the catch for many people with long COVID, POTS or MCAS. Several Mediterranean staples are foods that low-histamine diets usually exclude: fermented foods, aged cheese, cured meat, tomatoes, spinach and eggplant. Histamine in fish and meat also rises when freshness slips, which is why leftovers and canned or smoked fish are common triggers ([Comas-Basté et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom10081181)). You can keep the shape of the diet and swap the problem foods.

| Mediterranean staple | Why it can bother you | Lower-histamine swap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feta, parmesan and other aged cheese | Aged and fermented | Fresh mozzarella or ricotta, eaten fresh |
| Tomato sauce, sun-dried tomatoes | Commonly excluded | Olive oil, garlic and fresh herbs, or a roasted vegetable you tolerate |
| Spinach, eggplant | Commonly excluded | Zucchini, carrots or other vegetables you tolerate |
| Canned, smoked or not-quite-fresh fish | Histamine builds as freshness drops | Fresh or flash-frozen fish cooked from frozen |
| Yogurt, kefir, red wine, vinegar | Fermented | Fresh milk if you tolerate it, an olive oil and herb dressing, skip the wine |
| Strawberries, kiwi, pineapple | May trigger the body's own histamine release | Apples, pears, blueberries |
| Lentil stew kept in the fridge for days | Storage time | Freeze portions straight after cooking |

Two cautions. First, a strict low-histamine diet is a short experiment, not a way of life: our [low-histamine diet guide](/insights/food/low-histamine-diet-mcas/) covers how to run one and reintroduce foods, and [gentle low-histamine meal ideas](/insights/food/low-histamine-pots-meal-ideas/) has bad-day meals that fit both. Second, reactions are personal. A simple food and symptom log, on paper or in an app like Autonomic, is how you learn which swaps actually matter for you; see [how to find your triggers with a symptom journal](/insights/recovery/find-your-triggers-symptom-journal/).

## What about other long COVID diets?

Plenty of other diets get recommended for long COVID. Here is how the evidence stacks up.

| Diet | What it targets | Evidence in long COVID |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low-histamine | Histamine or MCAS-type reactions | No long COVID trials found; expert caution |
| Low-FODMAP | IBS-type gut symptoms | Strong in IBS, untested in long COVID |
| Ketogenic or low-carb | Energy metabolism (theory) | One case report |
| Fasting or very low calorie | Weight, metabolism | No fasting trials; one weight-loss trial |

**Low-histamine.** The British Dietetic Association has said there is a lack of evidence for low-histamine diets in long COVID and no research had tested them specifically; we found no trial since. It advises a short avoidance phase of about 2 to 4 weeks, then gradual reintroduction, with a dietitian, because long-term restriction risks nutrient gaps ([British Dietetic Association, 2021](https://www.bda.uk.com/resource/caution-advised-with-low-histamine-diets-for-long-covid.html)). Even outside long COVID, most low-histamine diet studies are small ([Comas-Basté et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom10081181)).

**Low-FODMAP.** For irritable bowel syndrome, the evidence is genuinely good: a network meta-analysis of 13 randomized trials with 944 patients ranked low-FODMAP first among the diets compared for overall symptoms ([Black et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1136/gutjnl-2021-325214)). It has not been tested in long COVID. If your main problem is bloating, pain or bowel changes, it may be worth doing with a dietitian, as a phase with reintroduction rather than for good.

**Ketogenic or low-carb.** The only published long COVID evidence we found is a single case report: one 44-year-old woman who improved on a 12-week program combining a ketogenic diet with supplements, sleep-rhythm and mindfulness work. The authors note a case report cannot show cause and effect, or which part helped ([Colgan et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.4236/crcm.2025.148052)).

**Fasting or very low calorie.** We found no published fasting trials in long COVID, and our piece on [intermittent fasting and POTS](/insights/food/intermittent-fasting-pots-hrv/) covers why skipping meals can backfire when your blood pressure is already unsteady. The closest trial is ReDIRECT: 234 adults with long COVID and excess weight (BMI above 27) were randomized to a remotely supported program starting with 850 calories a day of meal replacements for 12 weeks, or usual care. At six months the program group's main symptom had improved slightly more, by about a third of a point on the study's symptom scale ([Combet et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-03384-x)). That was supervised weight management for people with overweight, not a fasting plan, and it does not apply to anyone underweight or struggling to eat enough.

## The bottom line

An anti-inflammatory diet may modestly support long COVID recovery, and the Mediterranean diet is the best-studied way to do it. In long COVID it is linked to better metabolic health and a different gut microbiome, but the studies are observational, the classic inflammation markers did not budge in the one that checked, and no trial has tested it as a treatment. Make it cheap and low-effort, swap out the foods your body objects to, skip the extreme diets with almost no evidence, and judge any change over weeks of symptoms rather than one meal.
