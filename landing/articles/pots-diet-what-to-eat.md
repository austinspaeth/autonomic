If you have POTS, you have probably noticed that food can make or break an afternoon. A big lunch leaves you racing and foggy on the couch, while a salty snack can steady you. So what should you eat with POTS? **Smaller meals more often, salt and fluid on purpose, and carbs paired with protein, fat and fiber.** There is no single POTS diet, but those three habits do most of the work, and they fit ordinary family food.

This guide is the practical version: what the guidelines say about salt, a sample day of six small meals, salty snacks, what to limit, and what to eat when you are too tired to cook.

## Why does eating make POTS worse?

Digestion needs blood. After a meal, your body sends a large share of its blood to the stomach and intestines. A healthy system makes up for that by tightening blood vessels elsewhere. In POTS, where blood already tends to pool low in the body when you stand, that compensation falls short, so less blood returns to your heart and heart rate climbs to keep up. We explain the full mechanism in [why POTS feels worse after eating](/insights/pots/pots-worse-after-eating-postprandial-hypotension/).

A 2022 study measured this directly. Twelve women with POTS who said meals made them worse, and thirteen matched controls, drank 75 g of glucose. Standing heart rate rose about 21% in the POTS group and about 6% in controls, and the volume of blood pumped with each beat fell in POTS while it rose slightly in controls ([Breier et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35232225/)). The worsening tracked a gut hormone, GIP, that widens blood vessels in the gut. It was a small study, but it fits what many people feel after a large, sugary meal.

There is a second, slower effect. A fast rise in blood sugar from refined carbs can be followed by a dip a few hours later, and the adrenaline your body releases to correct it feels a lot like a POTS flare: shaky, sweaty, racing. That is covered in [blood sugar swings and POTS](/insights/food/reactive-hypoglycemia-blood-sugar-pots/). Gut symptoms are common in POTS too, and slow stomach emptying is the abnormality reported most often ([Mehr et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10286-018-0519-x)), which is another reason big plates can sit badly.

The practical answer to all three is the same: smaller meals, more often, built so they digest steadily. A dietitian writing for other dietitians lists exactly that, small frequent meals and mostly lower-glycemic carbs, as part of nutrition care in POTS ([Harris, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jand.2022.06.002)), and a 2023 clinical review notes that smaller meals make a post-meal blood pressure drop less likely ([Aboseif et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.3949/ccjm.90a.22093)).

## How much salt and fluid do POTS guidelines suggest?

This is where most diet pages go wrong, because they mix up salt and sodium. Table salt is sodium chloride. The World Health Organization's conversion is that 1 g of salt contains about 400 mg of sodium ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/salt-reduction)).

The two main POTS statements give their targets in grams of salt:

- The 2015 Heart Rhythm Society statement says up to 2 to 3 liters of water and 10 to 12 g of salt a day may be considered. It is a weak recommendation based on expert opinion ([Sheldon et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hrthm.2015.03.029)).
- The 2020 Canadian Cardiovascular Society statement recommends at least 3 liters of fluid and 10 g of salt a day, through salty foods, salt sachets, salt sticks or tablets. It calls this a strong recommendation on low-quality evidence ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024)).

So 10 to 12 g of salt is roughly 4,000 to 4,800 mg of sodium. For scale, the US Daily Value for sodium is under 2,300 mg ([FDA, 2021](https://www.fda.gov/media/84261/download)) and WHO advises adults to stay under 2,000 mg. The two statements differ a little on fluid (2 to 3 liters versus at least 3), but both land at roughly double the everyday salt limit.

Does it work? The best controlled test is small. In a crossover study, 14 people with POTS spent six days on a very high-sodium diet (300 mEq a day) and six on a very low one (10 mEq). On high sodium their plasma volume rose, standing norepinephrine fell and the jump in heart rate on standing shrank, though it stayed well above healthy controls ([Garland et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2021.03.005)). Salt helps, but it rarely fixes POTS on its own. Our [review of the salt and fluid evidence](/insights/research/science-of-salt-and-fluids-for-pots/) goes through what is proven and what is not, and the [POTS treatment guide](/insights/pots/pots-treatment-salt-fluids-compression-medication/) shows where diet sits next to compression, exercise and medication.

<div class="callout callout-warn"><strong>Salt is not safe for everyone.</strong> A high-salt diet can be harmful if you have high blood pressure, kidney disease or certain heart conditions, and it is not right for every person with POTS. A dietitian's guidance for POTS is to get the sodium goal from the medical team (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jand.2022.06.002">Harris, 2022</a>). Ask your clinician for your own number before you raise your intake.</div>

## How do I actually get enough salt?

Going from ordinary eating to around 10 g of salt is a big jump, so spread it out and build up. Where no target has been set, Harris suggests starting around 6 g of salt a day, divided through the day, and increasing slowly, partly because too much salt at once can bring on nausea. If you already crave salt, [why that happens in POTS](/insights/pots/why-do-i-crave-salt-pots/) is worth a read.

Practical ways to get there:

- **Salt your food, then a bit more.** Salting eggs, potatoes, rice and vegetables at the table is the cheapest source there is.
- **Drink broth.** A mug of chicken or beef broth carries salt and fluid together and is easy on a bad stomach. Check the label: "low sodium" broth defeats the purpose here.
- **Use salty sides.** Pickles, olives, cheese, salted nuts and canned soups add up fast.
- **Choose electrolyte drinks by sodium, not brand.** Many sports drinks are fairly low in sodium. Our guide to [electrolyte drinks for POTS](/insights/food/electrolyte-drinks-for-pots/) compares them and includes a homemade recipe.
- **Salt tablets only with a clinician.** The Canadian statement lists tablets as one option, but they are concentrated, so agree the dose with your clinician and take them with plenty of water.

Read Nutrition Facts labels to see where you are. The FDA counts 20% of the Daily Value or more per serving as high in sodium, which is 460 mg or more ([FDA, 2021](https://www.fda.gov/media/84261/download)). For POTS, those are the foods doing the heavy lifting.

Fluid matters as much as salt, since salt can only hold on to water you have drunk. Harris suggests starting fluids early, even before you get out of bed. A big glass of water drunk quickly can also raise blood pressure for a while through the [water bolus effect](/insights/pots/water-bolus-osmopressor-response-pots/), which is useful before a shower or a busy morning.

## What does a day of POTS-friendly meals look like?

Here is a sample day of six small meals built around salt, fluid, protein and slow carbs. It uses cheap, ordinary food a whole family can eat; the person with POTS simply salts more and drinks more.

<div class="table-wrap">

| When | What to eat | Salt and fluid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Breakfast** | Scrambled eggs, whole-grain toast with butter, a piece of fruit | Salt the eggs; a large glass of water, started before you get up |
| **Mid-morning** | Greek yogurt or cottage cheese with berries, or a handful of salted nuts | Electrolyte drink or water |
| **Lunch** | A mug of chicken noodle or bean soup with half a turkey or cheese sandwich | Broth gives salt and fluid together |
| **Afternoon** | A pickle spear, a cheese stick and whole-grain crackers | Water |
| **Dinner** | A modest plate: chicken thighs or salmon, rice or potatoes, a cooked vegetable with olive oil | Salt to taste, then a little more; water |
| **Evening** | Peanut butter on toast, or salted popcorn with a glass of milk | Water or milk |

</div>

Notice the pattern rather than the foods. Every meal has protein or fat next to its carbs, so sugar rises gently. No single meal is large. Salt and fluid show up six times, not once. Swap in what your family already eats: tacos with beans and salsa, rice bowls, leftovers from a roast. The olive oil, beans and fish here also overlap with the [Mediterranean diet](/insights/food/mediterranean-diet-long-covid/), if you want a broader eating pattern to lean on.

## What are good salty snacks for POTS?

Snacks are where much of the extra salt comes from. The best ones carry salt plus protein or fat, so they steady blood sugar as well as blood volume.

| Snack | Why it helps | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pickles and olives** | Very salty with no cooking. Dill pickles carry about 809 mg of sodium per 100 g ([USDA](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/168558/nutrients)) | Fermented kinds if you react to histamine |
| **Salted nuts or seeds** | Salt, protein and fat in one handful | Unsalted versions add nothing for volume |
| **Broth or cup-of-soup** | Salt and fluid together, warm and easy | Low-sodium versions |
| **Cheese and whole-grain crackers** | Protein slows the sugar rise | Aged cheeses if histamine is a trigger |
| **Cottage cheese or yogurt with fruit** | Protein, easy to eat cold | Sweetened yogurts add sugar fast |
| **Pretzels, chips, salted popcorn** | Quick salt | Mostly fast carbs: pair with cheese or nuts |

Sodium varies a lot by brand, so check the label of the version you buy rather than relying on a typical figure.

## Which foods should I limit with POTS?

There is no universal avoid list, and cutting out whole food groups usually makes eating harder without helping. These are the usual troublemakers:

- **Large meals.** Size matters more than any single ingredient, for the reasons above.
- **Refined carbs on their own.** A plain bagel, a big bowl of sweet cereal or a soda is a fast sugar load. Harris recommends fewer refined carbohydrates for steadier blood sugar. Pair carbs with protein instead of avoiding them.
- **Alcohol.** The HRS statement lists alcohol among the things that commonly make POTS symptoms worse, along with dehydration and heat.
- **Caffeine, for some people.** Harris advises avoiding it, but responses vary: some people race, others find a small coffee steadies them. Our [caffeine and POTS](/insights/pots/caffeine-and-pots-heart-rate-hrv/) article explains how to test your own.
- **High-histamine foods, only if you react.** If you also have mast cell symptoms, aged cheese, fermented foods, cured meats and leftovers can set off flares. Start with our [low-histamine POTS meal ideas](/insights/food/low-histamine-pots-meal-ideas/) rather than a strict elimination diet.

| Tends to help | Can make symptoms worse |
| --- | --- |
| Smaller meals, five or six a day | One or two large meals |
| Extra salt with plenty of fluid | Salt without enough water |
| Protein, fat and fiber with carbs | Refined carbs or sugar on their own |
| Water and electrolytes through the day | Alcohol |
| Broth, nuts, eggs, olives, cheese | Lots of caffeine, if you are sensitive |

Change one thing at a time and watch what happens over a week or two. A food you blame after one bad day may be innocent, since sleep, heat, your cycle and an infection all move symptoms too.

## What can I eat on low-energy days or if I'm picky?

Some days standing at the stove is out of the question, and a tired or queasy stomach narrows what you can face. Low-effort food still counts.

- **No-cook plates.** Cheese, crackers, pickles, sliced turkey and an apple. Nothing to heat.
- **Drinkable meals.** Broth in a mug, a smoothie made with milk or yogurt, or a glass of milk. Many people find liquids easier to manage than a full plate on a bad day.
- **Ready-made protein.** Hard-boiled eggs, rotisserie chicken, canned tuna or salmon, cottage cheese.
- **Freezer portions.** On a good day, cook soup or chili and freeze single servings.
- **For picky eaters.** Start with salty foods you already like: buttered toast with extra salt, a cheese quesadilla, salted rice, mashed potatoes. Variety can come later.

Keep a snack and a drink beside your bed or on the couch, so getting salt and fluid does not depend on getting up.

### A short shopping list

- **Salt and fluid:** broth or bouillon, electrolyte mix, pickles, olives, canned soups, table salt
- **Protein:** eggs, chicken thighs, canned tuna or salmon, cheese sticks, cottage cheese, Greek yogurt, peanut butter, salted nuts
- **Slow carbs:** whole-grain bread and crackers, oats, rice, potatoes, beans, popcorn kernels
- **Fruit and vegetables:** apples, bananas, berries (frozen is fine), frozen vegetables, carrots
- **Fats:** olive oil, butter

If you want to know which of these actually steady you, a food and symptom journal helps, because post-meal symptoms tend to show up later and get blamed on the wrong thing. Autonomic lets you log meals, salt and water next to your readings for exactly this.

## The bottom line

What you eat with POTS matters less than how much and how often. Eat smaller meals five or six times a day, pair carbs with protein and fat, and add salt and fluid on purpose. The guidelines talk about roughly 10 g of salt (about 4,000 mg of sodium) and 2 to 3 liters of fluid or more, but your number should come from a clinician who knows your blood pressure, kidneys and heart. Limit large meals, refined carbs on their own and alcohol, keep easy salty food within reach for bad days, and change one thing at a time.
