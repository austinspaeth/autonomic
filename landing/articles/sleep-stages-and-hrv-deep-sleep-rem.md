## Your morning HRV is a report card on the night

If you measure heart rate variability first thing after waking, it is tempting to read the number as a forecast: a verdict on the day ahead. It is closer to a report card on the night behind. Most of your parasympathetic restoration happens while you sleep, and **sleep stages and HRV** are tightly linked: your nervous system does not rest evenly from lights-out to sunrise. It swings between a deeply calm state and a surprisingly busy one, several times a night, and your morning reading is mostly a summary of how those swings went.

Understanding which stage does what turns a jumpy morning number into something you can actually read. It explains why a short night hits harder than you would expect, why you sometimes wake with a pounding heart in the small hours, and why the best thing you can do for tomorrow's HRV happens before midnight, not after breakfast.

REM is the opposite, a sympathetically active state where heart rate rises and gets irregular. Deep sleep loads into the first half of the night; REM lengthens toward morning. Lose sleep off either end and you mostly lose recovery.

## What your nervous system does as you sleep

A night of sleep is built from repeating cycles, each roughly 90 minutes long, and each cycle moves through a sequence of stages. Clinically they are grouped as non-REM (further split into light stages N1 and N2, and deep stage N3) and REM, the stage where most dreaming happens. You pass through four to six of these cycles in a typical night, but the *mix* within them shifts as the night goes on.

The autonomic story across those stages is clean and well established:

- **Light sleep (N1, N2)** is the transition. Heart rate drifts down, muscles relax, and the parasympathetic branch starts taking over. HRV rises above its awake-but-resting level.
- **Deep sleep (N3, slow-wave sleep)** is the restorative core. The vagus nerve dominates, heart rate and blood pressure fall to their overnight lows, and HRV climbs to its daily peak. This is the window where growth hormone is released and a lot of physical repair happens.
- **REM sleep** breaks the calm. The brain becomes highly active, dreams turn vivid, and there are bursts of sympathetic ("fight or flight") activity. Heart rate rises and becomes irregular from beat to beat, blood pressure swings, and HRV drops and gets erratic.

So within a single cycle you go from a parasympathetic high (deep sleep) to a sympathetic flare (REM), and then start over. The reason your overnight recovery is so sensitive to *when* you sleep is that these stages are not evenly distributed, which we will come to shortly.

## Which sleep stage has the highest HRV?

Deep sleep, every time. Slow-wave sleep is the most parasympathetically dominant state of your entire 24 hours, more than any waking meditation or breathing session can reach. Your heart rate settles to its true [overnight low](/insights/basics/overnight-heart-rate-while-you-sleep/), the beat-to-beat flicker driven by the vagus nerve is at its strongest, and time-domain metrics like [RMSSD](/insights/basics/rmssd-and-pnn50-vagal-tone-metrics/) along with high-frequency power sit at their nightly peak.

REM anchors the other end. Despite your body lying still, the autonomic system is anything but calm, so HRV in REM is low and noisy in a way that reflects sympathetic activity rather than vagal tone. Light sleep lands in the middle, and lying awake in bed, even relaxed, usually sits below deep sleep because your system never fully hands over to the parasympathetic side while you are conscious.

This is also why average heart rate can hide what HRV reveals. Two nights can share the same mean heart rate, but the one with more undisturbed deep sleep will produce a visibly higher morning HRV, because the quality of the recovery, not just the pace of the heart, is what the metric captures.

## Why REM spikes your heart rate

REM deserves its own paragraph because it surprises people. You are lying still, deeply asleep, yet your heart rate can rise, stutter, and during the most vivid dreams briefly approach the kind of numbers you would see walking around. Blood pressure jumps too. This is not a malfunction. REM is a state of high brain activity and genuine autonomic instability, with phasic surges of sympathetic outflow that are part of normal physiology.

It matters for this community for two reasons. First, REM periods get longer in the second half of the night, so the early-morning hours are the busiest, most sympathetically active stretch of your sleep. That timing overlaps with the natural pre-dawn rise in cortisol and is part of why so many cardiovascular events, and so many [early-morning symptom flares](/insights/pots/pots-symptoms-worse-in-the-morning/), cluster around waking. Second, in a hyperadrenergic nervous system the REM surge lands on an already-primed system, which is one common explanation for waking at 4 or 5 a.m. with a racing heart and a jolt of adrenaline, unable to settle back down.

## The shape of a night, and why the first half matters most

Here is the detail that ties everything together: **deep sleep is front-loaded.** The first one or two cycles of the night contain most of your slow-wave sleep. As the night continues, deep sleep shrinks and REM expands. By the final cycles before waking, you may get almost no deep sleep at all and long stretches of REM.

The practical consequence is pointed. **A night cut short usually costs you the wrong half.** If you go to bed two hours late but wake at your normal time, you have not shaved a little off each stage evenly, you have mostly removed deep sleep from the front of the night, which is precisely the parasympathetic recovery window. That is why a single late night can tank the next morning's HRV while a night where you woke early but went to bed on time often looks surprisingly fine. It is also why the advice to "protect your bedtime" is not nagging: the first hours are where the recovery lives.

## How POTS, long COVID and dysautonomia change the picture

Dysautonomia complicates every part of this. People with POTS, long COVID and related conditions frequently report unrefreshing sleep, and the overnight data often backs it up: less deep sleep, more fragmentation, and a nervous system that stays partly switched on when it should be winding down. A system biased toward sympathetic activity has a harder time dropping into slow-wave sleep in the first place, so the most restorative stage gets squeezed from both sides.

A few patterns show up often:

- **"Wired but tired."** You are exhausted but cannot fall or stay asleep, because the sympathetic dial is stuck too high at bedtime. We dig into this in [POTS insomnia](/insights/pots/pots-insomnia-wired-but-tired/).
- **Early-morning adrenaline wake-ups.** The natural REM surge in the second half of the night, amplified by a hyperadrenergic state, pulls you out of sleep with a racing heart.
- **A flat [nocturnal dip](/insights/basics/non-dipping-blood-pressure-nocturnal-dip/).** Normally heart rate and blood pressure fall overnight; when the autonomic system never fully stands down, that dip shrinks, which is a meaningful signal in its own right.
- **Hidden [sleep apnea](/insights/hrv/sleep-apnea-and-hrv-dysautonomia/).** Breathing interruptions trigger repeated sympathetic surges all night and are easy to miss, and they will flatten overnight HRV no matter how disciplined your sleep hygiene is.

None of this means your sleep is broken beyond repair. It means the overnight numbers are worth watching, because they are often the earliest place a good or bad stretch shows up, and because improving sleep tends to move HRV further than almost any supplement.

## How wearables estimate stages, and how far to trust them

Your watch or ring did not read your brainwaves. A sleep lab stages sleep with EEG, measuring electrical activity directly. A wearable infers stages from heart rate, HRV, breathing and movement. That inference is genuinely good at separating asleep from awake and gives a reasonable overall shape of the night, but the stage breakdown, especially the split between deep and REM, is an estimate and can be well off on any given night.

The takeaway is not to ignore the data, it is to read it at the right resolution. Do not agonize over "only 38 minutes of deep sleep last night." Do watch whether your deep sleep, overnight low heart rate and morning HRV are trending together over two or three weeks. Trends survive the noise that single nights do not. This is the same principle that governs HRV itself, covered in the [complete HRV guide](/insights/hrv/hrv-complete-guide/): one reading is weather, a two-week slope is climate.

## How to read your own overnight numbers

You do not need lab staging to get real value from your sleep data. A handful of numbers, read together, tell a coherent story.

| What to look at | Where it comes from | What a good night looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Morning HRV (RMSSD) | First reading after waking | At or above your personal baseline, trending steady or up |
| Overnight low heart rate | Your lowest sleeping heart rate | A clear dip well below your daytime resting rate |
| Nocturnal dip | Day vs night heart rate and BP | Heart rate and blood pressure both fall overnight |
| Deep sleep amount | Wearable estimate | Present in the first cycles, trending stable week to week |
| Awake time / arousals | Wearable estimate | Low and not clustered in the early morning |

Read as a set, these line up. A night with solid deep sleep usually shows a low overnight heart rate, a healthy dip, and a morning HRV at or above baseline. A fragmented, REM-heavy, sympathetically busy night shows the opposite: a higher overnight low, a shallow dip, and a suppressed morning number. When they disagree, that is informative too, and often the first clue of something like apnea or an oncoming illness.

## What actually improves deep sleep and overnight HRV

The interventions that reliably help are unglamorous, which is good news, because they are also free and repeatable:

- **Protect the first hours.** A consistent bedtime matters more than a consistent wake time, because the front of the night is where deep sleep lives. Going to bed at the same time most nights is the single highest-leverage habit here.
- **Wind the sympathetic dial down before bed.** A few minutes of slow breathing at around six breaths per minute actively raises vagal tone and eases the transition into sleep. It is the same mechanism behind [resonant breathing](/insights/hrv/resonant-breathing-hrv-biofeedback/), and it is a genuinely calming pre-sleep ritual.
- **Mind the obvious disruptors.** Alcohol in particular fragments the second half of the night and blunts HRV; late heavy meals, late caffeine and a hot bedroom all cost you deep sleep.
- **Treat sleep as pacing.** A poor night narrows the next day's energy envelope, so resting after a bad night is not weakness, it is [staying inside the envelope](/insights/recovery/pacing-101-energy-envelope/) instead of trading one rough night for a multi-day crash.
- **Rule out apnea if the numbers stay stuck.** If your overnight low stays high and your morning HRV refuses to climb despite good habits, ask a clinician about a sleep study.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which sleep stage has the highest HRV?** Deep sleep (slow-wave sleep, N3). It is the most parasympathetically dominant state of the whole day, so heart rate hits its overnight low and HRV reaches its nightly peak. REM has the lowest and most erratic HRV of the sleep stages.

**Why does my heart rate spike during REM sleep?** REM is an autonomically active state with bursts of sympathetic outflow, so heart rate rises and becomes irregular and blood pressure swings, even though you are lying still. In a hyperadrenergic nervous system this surge is exaggerated, which is a common reason for early-morning wake-ups with a racing heart.

**Does deep sleep increase HRV?** Yes. More undisturbed deep sleep means a stronger overnight parasympathetic recovery window and generally a higher morning HRV. Because deep sleep is front-loaded into the first half of the night, protecting your bedtime moves HRV more than anything you do after waking.

**How accurate are sleep stages from a watch or ring?** Good at asleep versus awake and reasonable for total sleep time, but the stage breakdown is an estimate from heart rate, HRV and movement rather than a brainwave recording. Hold a single night's exact minutes loosely and trust the multi-week trend.

## The bottom line

Sleep stages and HRV move together in a predictable rhythm: deep sleep is the parasympathetic peak where heart rate bottoms out and HRV climbs highest, and REM is the sympathetically active counterweight where both get noisy. Because deep sleep is concentrated in the first half of the night, your morning HRV is largely a measure of how much undisturbed early-night deep sleep you got. That is good news in the plainest sense: the most effective thing you can do for tomorrow's number is protect tonight's bedtime, not chase the reading after you wake. Watch your overnight low, your nocturnal dip and your deep-sleep trend alongside your HRV, follow them over weeks rather than nights, and the morning number stops being a mystery and starts being a map.
