Your **respiratory rate**, how many breaths you take in a minute, is one of the oldest vital signs in medicine and one of the most useful. It is also the one nobody writes down. Heart rate, blood pressure and temperature get measured at every visit; breathing rate often gets estimated in a glance or skipped. That is a shame, because for someone recovering from long COVID, POTS or another form of dysautonomia, the way you breathe at rest carries real information, and it tends to change early.

This is educational field notes, not medical advice. The goal here is to help you understand and track your own breathing, not to diagnose anything.

## What is a normal respiratory rate?

For an adult at rest and awake, the widely used clinical range is **12 to 20 breaths per minute**. Many healthy, rested people actually sit a little tighter than that, somewhere around 12 to 16. A breath is one full cycle, in and out, so if you count 14 inhales in a minute, your respiratory rate is 14.

A few things move the number legitimately: activity, fever, pain, a full stomach, caffeine, altitude, and plain anxiety all push it up, while deep rest and sleep tend to settle it. Children breathe considerably faster than adults, so the ranges below are for grown-ups.

| Context | Typical rate (breaths per minute) |
| --- | --- |
| Adult, relaxed and well rested | 12 to 16 |
| Adult, awake at rest (standard range) | 12 to 20 |
| Adult, asleep | usually steady near your own baseline, often 12 to 20 |
| Fast (tachypnea) | above 20 to 24 at rest |
| Slow (bradypnea) | below 12 at rest |
| Child, 6 to 12 years (for context only) | 18 to 30 |

## Why respiratory rate is the vital sign nobody checks

Part of the reason breathing rate gets skipped is practical: it is tedious to measure well, and the moment people know you are counting their breaths, they change how they breathe. But there is a better reason to pay attention to it. Of the standard vital signs, respiratory rate is often the **earliest to shift** when something is changing, whether that is a brewing infection or a nervous system sliding out of balance.

Wearable makers noticed this during the pandemic: an overnight respiratory rate creeping above your own usual was, for many people, one of the first measurable hints of an illness a day or two before symptoms. The absolute number a ring or watch reports is only an estimate, but the *direction* relative to your baseline is genuinely informative. That is the whole theme of this article, and of [tracking recovery in general](/insights/basics/hrv-vs-resting-heart-rate/): your own stable baseline beats any textbook range.

## How breathing rate and HRV are linked

If you track [heart rate variability](/insights/basics/rmssd-and-pnn50-vagal-tone-metrics/), respiratory rate is not a separate story, it is part of the same one. Your heart speeds up slightly as you breathe in and slows as you breathe out. That rhythmic swing is called **respiratory sinus arrhythmia**, and it is one of the main things short-term HRV measures. We have a [whole article on respiratory sinus arrhythmia](/insights/hrv/respiratory-sinus-arrhythmia-breathing-hrv/) if you want the mechanism in detail.

The practical consequence is simple and a little surprising. **How fast you breathe largely sets how much beat-to-beat HRV you show.** Slow, deep breaths give the heart more time to accelerate and decelerate within each cycle, so the swing is large and HRV reads high. Fast, shallow breaths chop the cycle short, the swing shrinks, and HRV reads low. Two people with identical hearts can post very different HRV numbers purely because one is breathing at six breaths a minute and the other at eighteen.

This is the engine behind [resonant breathing](/insights/hrv/resonant-breathing-hrv-biofeedback/), the practice of slowing to roughly five or six breaths a minute to maximize that swing. It is also why a chronically high respiratory rate and a stubbornly low HRV so often appear together: they are two views of the same over-revved state.

## Why respiratory rate runs high in long COVID and POTS

If your resting breathing rate reads higher than you expected, you are in common company. Several mechanisms stack up in post-viral illness and dysautonomia.

### An over-revved nervous system

[POTS and dysautonomia](/insights/pots/is-it-pots-or-anxiety/) involve a nervous system tilted toward its sympathetic, fight-or-flight branch, with the calming vagal brake relatively weak. Faster breathing is part of that sympathetic package, the same way a racing heart and a jittery, wired feeling are. The breathing is not separate from the autonomic picture; it is one of its signs.

### Deconditioning

A long illness, and the bed rest that often comes with it, shrinks your aerobic capacity. With less cardiovascular reserve, ordinary effort (standing, a flight of stairs, carrying groceries) demands proportionally more, so you breathe faster and feel winded sooner. This overlaps heavily with, but is not the same as, true [exercise intolerance and post-exertional malaise](/insights/postviral/post-exertional-malaise-explained/).

### Dysfunctional breathing

Here is the one that surprises people most. A **breathing pattern disorder**, fast, shallow, upper-chest breathing instead of slow, diaphragmatic breaths, is genuinely common after COVID and in dysautonomia. It can linger long after the lungs themselves have recovered. The trouble is that over-breathing blows off too much carbon dioxide, and low CO2 (hypocapnia) is itself what produces much of the [air hunger](/insights/postviral/air-hunger-long-covid-pots/), tingling, chest tightness and lightheadedness. So the sensation of not getting enough air drives faster breathing, which lowers CO2 further, which worsens the sensation. It is a loop, and the way out usually runs through slowing down, not speeding up.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <strong>Fast and shallow is not the same as low oxygen.</strong> In dysfunctional breathing, your blood oxygen is usually fine; the problem is too little carbon dioxide from over-breathing, which paradoxically makes you feel short of air. A <a href="/insights/postviral/pulse-oximeter-spo2-long-covid-pots/">pulse oximeter</a> that reads a healthy 97 to 99 percent while you still feel breathless is a strong hint that the issue is breathing pattern rather than the lungs, though a genuinely low reading always deserves attention.
</div>

## Reading your overnight respiratory rate

The single most useful way to use respiratory rate is not to compare yourself to a chart, it is to learn **your own baseline** and watch for drift. Overnight is ideal, because you are still, you are not consciously controlling your breath, and conditions are consistent night to night. Most rings and watches estimate a nightly respiratory rate for exactly this reason.

A stable baseline with the occasional small wobble is normal. What you are watching for is a clear step up that holds for a night or two, because that is where the signal lives: an early infection, a flare, a stretch of poor sleep, or a slide back into shallow breathing.

## How to measure your respiratory rate

**By hand.** Sit quietly for a few minutes first. The classic trick is to count without thinking about it, because awareness changes your breathing. One reliable method: after a rest, count breaths for 30 seconds and double it, or watch the rise and fall of your chest for a full minute. Rest a hand on your upper belly; a calm breath should move the belly more than the upper chest.

**With a wearable.** Rings and watches estimate your overnight rate from respiratory sinus arrhythmia, the beat-to-beat heart-rate ripple that breathing creates, sometimes aided by motion sensors. Treat the exact figure as an estimate and the night-to-night trend as the real tool.

| Raises respiratory rate | Lowers respiratory rate |
| --- | --- |
| Activity, exertion, standing up | Rest and sleep |
| Fever, infection, illness | Recovery, calm |
| Anxiety, panic, stress | Slow, nasal, diaphragmatic breathing |
| Caffeine and stimulants | Being well hydrated and rested |
| Deconditioning after illness | Improved aerobic fitness over time |
| Pain, high altitude | Returning to sea level, pain relief |

## When an elevated respiratory rate needs a clinician

Most of the time, a slightly fast resting rate is a trend to watch, not an emergency. But breathing rate is a vital sign precisely because it can flag something serious, so a few patterns deserve prompt attention rather than tracking.

<div class="callout callout-warn">
  <strong>Seek medical care, not an app, for these.</strong> A resting respiratory rate persistently above 20 to 24 breaths per minute, especially with real breathlessness, chest pain, a pulse-oximeter reading that keeps dropping below about 94 percent, bluish lips or fingertips, confusion, or breathing that is getting rapidly worse. A rate above 24 with distress is a recognized warning sign. New or worsening shortness of breath always warrants evaluation. These notes are educational and cannot assess you; a clinician can.
</div>

For a slow upward drift without those red flags, the right move is usually gentler: note it, look at your trend, and raise it at your next appointment with your numbers in hand.

## How to bring a fast breathing rate down

If the issue is pattern rather than a lung or heart problem, the fix is mostly retraining, and it tends to help HRV at the same time.

- **Breathe through your nose,** slowly, letting the belly move rather than the upper chest. Nasal breathing naturally slows the rate and improves CO2 tolerance.
- **Practice slow breathing** at roughly five to six breaths a minute for a few minutes a day. This is [resonant breathing](/insights/hrv/resonant-breathing-hrv-biofeedback/), and it directly widens the heart-rate swing that HRV measures.
- **Rebuild capacity gently,** within your [energy envelope](/insights/recovery/pacing-101-energy-envelope/), so that deconditioning does not keep forcing fast breathing at low effort. Pace, do not push into a crash.
- **Treat the trigger, not just the breath.** If anxiety, pain, poor sleep or an infection is driving the rate, that is where the leverage is.

A breathing-pattern problem that will not budge, or one tangled up with real breathlessness, is worth a referral to a respiratory physiotherapist or a clinician who can assess it properly.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a normal respiratory rate?** For an adult at rest and awake, the standard range is 12 to 20 breaths per minute, and many rested people sit around 12 to 16. Children breathe faster, and the number rises with activity, fever, pain and stress. Your own resting baseline over weeks is more useful than any single figure.

**Why is my respiratory rate high in long COVID or POTS?** Usually a stack of causes: a sympathetically tilted nervous system, deconditioning after illness, and a breathing pattern disorder (fast, shallow, upper-chest breathing) that lowers CO2 and feeds air hunger. It is common and often improvable, and a persistent elevation is worth discussing with a clinician.

**What respiratory rate is concerning?** A resting rate that stays above 20 to 24, especially with breathlessness, low oxygen, chest pain, confusion or bluish lips, needs prompt medical care. A rate above 24 with distress is a recognized warning sign. Slow drifts without those signs are something to track and raise at an appointment.

**How does breathing rate affect HRV?** Through respiratory sinus arrhythmia: the heart speeds on the inhale and slows on the exhale. Slow, deep breathing widens that swing and raises short-term HRV; fast, shallow breathing flattens it. That is why resonant breathing boosts HRV and why fast breathing and low HRV tend to coexist.

**How do wearables measure respiratory rate while I sleep?** Indirectly, mostly from the beat-to-beat heart-rate ripple that breathing creates, sometimes with motion data. The absolute number is an estimate, so use it as a trend tool against your own baseline rather than as an exact clinical value.

## The bottom line

Respiratory rate is a quiet, powerful vital sign. For an adult at rest it runs 12 to 20 breaths per minute, often 12 to 16 when you are truly relaxed, and it climbs early when something is changing. In long COVID and POTS it frequently runs fast and shallow, both reflecting an over-revved nervous system and dragging your HRV down through the same respiratory-sinus-arrhythmia link that slow breathing uses to lift it. Learn your own baseline, watch for a step up that holds, favor slow nasal breathing, and bring any persistent change (or any real breathlessness) to a clinician. The number is yours to track; the interpretation of a worrying trend belongs with someone who can examine you.
