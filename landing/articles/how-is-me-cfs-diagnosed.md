If you suspect you have ME/CFS, the diagnosis can feel like a moving target. You might have heard there is no test, or that you have to wait six months, or that only a specialist can diagnose it. **ME/CFS is diagnosed on symptoms, because no blood test or scan can confirm it yet. A clinician looks for a specific pattern, above all post-exertional malaise, after routine tests rule out other causes. US criteria ask for six months of symptoms; UK guidance allows a diagnosis at three.** Here is what the criteria say, what tests to expect, and how to make the appointment count.

## Is there a test for ME/CFS?

Not yet. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says there is no confirmatory test for ME/CFS. People with it often have measurable biological differences, but none is accurate enough to diagnose it on its own ([CDC, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/diagnosis/index.html)). The UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) tells clinicians to explain this up front: ME/CFS is recognised on clinical grounds alone ([NICE, 2021](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206)).

That doesn't make it a diagnosis of "nothing else found". The criteria describe a specific set of symptoms, and the one that sets ME/CFS apart is post-exertional malaise (PEM): a worsening of symptoms after physical, mental or emotional effort, often delayed by a day or two and lasting days or longer. If you are not sure whether what you get is PEM, [post-exertional malaise, explained](/insights/postviral/post-exertional-malaise-explained/) walks through how it shows up.

## What criteria do doctors use?

In the US, the CDC uses the criteria from the 2015 Institute of Medicine (IOM) report, now the National Academy of Medicine ([Institute of Medicine, 2015](https://doi.org/10.17226/19012)). You need all three of these:

- **A substantial drop in what you can do** at work, school, home or socially, lasting more than 6 months, with fatigue that is often profound, new, not caused by overdoing it and not much relieved by rest.
- **Post-exertional malaise.**
- **Unrefreshing sleep.**

Plus at least one of:

- **Cognitive impairment,** such as problems with memory, attention or processing speed.
- **Orthostatic intolerance,** symptoms that worsen when you stand or sit upright and ease when you lie down.

The symptoms must be present at least half the time, at moderate or greater intensity ([CDC, IOM 2015 criteria](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/diagnosis/iom-2015-diagnostic-criteria-1.html)). A 2021 paper from 21 ME/CFS clinicians notes that the CDC has adopted these criteria, and lays out how general doctors can use them ([Bateman et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mayocp.2021.07.004)).

The UK works differently. NICE's 2021 guideline lists four symptoms that must all be present: debilitating fatigue made worse by activity, PEM, unrefreshing or disturbed sleep, and cognitive difficulties, along with a big drop in function and no other explanation. Clinicians should suspect ME/CFS once these have lasted 6 weeks in adults (4 weeks in children) and diagnose it at 3 months ([NICE, 2021](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206)).

You may also hear of two older, more detailed sets of criteria. The Canadian Consensus Criteria from 2003 build the diagnosis around patterns of symptom clusters and are often used in research ([Carruthers et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1300/J092v11n01_02)). The International Consensus Criteria from 2011 make post-exertional "neuroimmune exhaustion" compulsory, require symptoms across several body systems, and drop the 6-month wait entirely ([Carruthers et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2796.2011.02428.x)).

| Criteria | Core symptoms required | When it can be diagnosed |
|---|---|---|
| IOM 2015 (used by the CDC) | Reduced function with fatigue, PEM, unrefreshing sleep, plus brain fog or orthostatic intolerance | After more than 6 months |
| NICE 2021 (UK) | Fatigue, PEM, unrefreshing sleep and brain fog, with reduced function | Suspect at 6 weeks, diagnose at 3 months |
| International Consensus 2011 | Post-exertional exhaustion plus symptoms across several body systems | No minimum wait |

## What tests will they run?

Because there is no test for ME/CFS itself, the tests are there to rule out other illnesses that cause fatigue. The CDC lists the tests ME/CFS specialists routinely use, though not all are needed at once ([CDC, 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/diagnosis-testing/evaluation-of-me-cfs.html)):

- **Blood count and inflammation:** complete blood count, ESR and C-reactive protein.
- **Chemistry:** electrolytes, fasting glucose, kidney and liver function, calcium, phosphate and protein.
- **Thyroid:** TSH and free T4.
- **Iron studies:** to check for both deficiency and overload.
- **Celiac screening and a urine test.**

NICE's list is similar and adds HbA1c and creatine kinase ([NICE, 2021](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206)). Some ME/CFS clinicians also check vitamin B12, folate and vitamin D.

Most people with ME/CFS get routine results within normal ranges, despite being very unwell ([CDC, 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/diagnosis-testing/evaluation-of-me-cfs.html)). Normal results don't mean nothing is wrong. They mean the common look-alikes have been checked. Depending on your symptoms, your clinician may also suggest a sleep study, imaging or a tilt table test. If standing makes you worse, it is worth asking about orthostatic intolerance directly, since it counts toward the diagnosis and often overlaps with POTS; [how POTS is diagnosed](/insights/pots/how-pots-is-diagnosed/) explains what that test involves.

## What about the two-day exercise test?

A two-day cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET) measures how much oxygen your body uses during a maximal cycling test, then repeats it 24 hours later. Healthy people usually match their first result. In a study of 22 people with ME/CFS, their peak oxygen use fell by 13.8% on day two and the workload they could manage at their ventilatory threshold fell by 21.3%. Relying on one test alone would have overestimated function for half of them ([Keller et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/1479-5876-12-104)). Across 32 studies, people with ME/CFS also had lower peak oxygen uptake than healthy controls even on a single test ([Franklin et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-0802-9175)).

That makes it the closest thing to an objective measure of PEM. But none of the main criteria require it, it is available mainly at specialist and research centres, and it works by provoking the very crash it measures. Researchers have proposed it as a way to document how limited someone is, for example for a disability claim, more than as a way to make the diagnosis.

<div class="callout callout-note"><strong>Ask before you agree to any exercise test.</strong> A maximal test, one day or two, can leave someone with ME/CFS worse for days or longer. If one is suggested, ask what it will change about your care and how you will be supported afterwards.</div>

## Who diagnoses ME/CFS?

Any clinician can, using the CDC or NICE criteria. In the UK, NICE recommends that a GP who suspects ME/CFS refer adults to a specialist ME/CFS team to confirm the diagnosis and set up a care plan, and that children see a paediatrician first ([NICE, 2021](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206)). The US has no referral pathway like this and few ME/CFS specialists, so you may be diagnosed by a primary care doctor or internist, or seen in a long COVID or dysautonomia clinic.

Getting there can take a while. The CDC lists the reasons: no lab test, symptoms shared with many illnesses, patients who don't look ill at appointments, symptoms that come and go, and some clinicians who don't accept ME/CFS as real ([CDC, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/diagnosis-testing/factors-complicating-the-diagnosis-of-me-cfs.html)). The 2021 clinician consensus paper estimated that up to 91% of people with ME/CFS in the US are undiagnosed ([Bateman et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mayocp.2021.07.004)). In a 2021 to 2022 national survey, 1.3% of US adults said a health professional had told them they had ME/CFS and they still had it, with women (1.7%) more likely than men (0.9%) ([Vahratian et al., 2023](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db488.htm)).

If your symptoms started after COVID, the path can overlap with long COVID care. [Is long COVID the same as ME/CFS?](/insights/mecfs/is-long-covid-the-same-as-me-cfs/) covers how the two relate and how often one meets the criteria for the other.

## What should you bring to the appointment?

The diagnosis rests almost entirely on your history, so the clearer it is, the better. The CDC suggests clinicians ask questions like these, and even ask patients to keep a diary of activities and symptoms for a couple of weeks ([CDC, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/diagnosis/index.html)):

- What can you do now, compared with before you got sick?
- How much physical or mental activity does it take to make you feel ill, and how long does recovery take?
- How do you feel after sleeping or resting?
- What happens when you stand or sit upright for a while?
- What do you now avoid because of what happens afterwards?

Come with your own answers. A diary is especially useful for PEM, because the crash often arrives a day or two after the effort and is easy to miss in a 15-minute appointment. Write down what you did and how you felt over the next two days, and note any times you felt worse after standing. [Keeping a symptom journal](/insights/recovery/find-your-triggers-symptom-journal/) covers how to set one up so patterns show. Some researchers use a short questionnaire about how long PEM lasts: in one study, five questions from the DePaul Symptom Questionnaire about PEM duration identified people with ME/CFS correctly 81.7% of the time ([Cotler et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/diagnostics8030066)).

Bring a short summary too: when it started, what your life looked like before, your worst symptoms, and what you have already tried. If you are pacing, [what heart rate to stay under](/insights/mecfs/what-heart-rate-should-i-stay-under-when-pacing/) explains a simple way to track it that also gives your doctor numbers to look at.

## The bottom line

There is no single test for ME/CFS. It is diagnosed on symptoms, above all post-exertional malaise, along with a big drop in function, unrefreshing sleep and either brain fog or orthostatic intolerance, after routine tests rule out other causes. US criteria ask for symptoms lasting more than 6 months, while UK guidance allows a diagnosis at 3 months. Most people get normal blood results, and a two-day exercise test is optional and carries a real risk of a crash. A diary that shows what you did and how you felt a day or two later is one of the most useful things you can bring.

<div class="callout callout-warn"><strong>Not medical advice.</strong> This article is educational and does not replace care from a clinician who knows your history. Fatigue has many causes, some of them serious, so see a doctor to rule them out. Talk to your doctor before changing your activity, treatment or medication. If your symptoms are new, worsening or frightening, get medical care.</div>
