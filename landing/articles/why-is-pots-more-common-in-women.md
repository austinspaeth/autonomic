If you have POTS and you are a woman, you have probably noticed that almost everyone you meet with it is too. That is not a coincidence or a quirk of who joins online groups. **POTS is far more common in women, mostly between their teens and forties, and the research points to several things stacking up: lower blood volume and stroke volume for body size, less blood returning to the heart when you stand, and hormones that change how much fluid the body holds on to across the cycle.** No single cause has been proven, but here is what the studies actually show, and what it means for you.

## How much more common is POTS in women?

A lot more. In the largest survey of people with POTS, 94% of 4,835 respondents were female, and about half first had symptoms as teenagers ([Shaw et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.12895)). A later survey of 8,919 people found almost the same split, 93.7% female ([Bourne et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjco.2021.08.014)). Surveys recruit through patient communities, so they can over-count anyone more likely to join one, but clinic studies point the same way: in a Mayo Clinic follow-up of people seen in clinic, 84% were women ([Kimpinski et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mayocp.2012.02.020)).

The Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) puts it at more than 90% female, with most people presenting between the ages of 13 and 40 ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024)). That age window matters, because it lines up with the years from puberty to the late reproductive years, which is one reason researchers look so closely at hormones.

## What is different about how women's bodies handle standing?

Standing pulls blood down into your legs and belly, and your body has to push enough back up to keep the heart filled and your brain supplied. Even without POTS, young women find this harder than men. A 2025 review pulled the research together: after adjusting for body size, women have lower blood volume and lower stroke volume (the amount of blood the heart pumps per beat), and when upright they get less help from the leg muscle pump and the breathing pump that push blood back toward the heart ([Fitzgibbon-Collins et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.autneu.2025.103284)). Their blood vessels also respond less strongly to the nerve signals that tell them to tighten, and they have more capacity to widen.

A lab study shows how that plays out. Researchers used a chamber that pulls blood toward the legs, much like standing does, and increased it until people nearly fainted. Healthy young women tolerated about a third less than men ([Fu et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpheart.00735.2002)). Near fainting, the women's stroke volume was 35% lower and their heart rates were higher, while blood vessel tightening and stress hormone levels were similar to the men's. The authors concluded the difference came from less blood refilling the heart, not from weaker blood vessel responses.

## Do people with POTS have smaller hearts and less blood?

On average, the evidence says yes. In a study of 27 people with POTS compared with healthy controls, the POTS group had a smaller left ventricle (the heart's main pumping chamber) for their body weight and less blood: a median of 60 ml per kg compared with 71 ml per kg ([Fu et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2010.02.043)). When they stood, their stroke volume was lower and their heart rate higher. The researchers called it a small heart coupled with reduced blood volume. For how low blood volume drives the racing heart, see [low blood volume in POTS](/insights/pots/low-blood-volume-pots-hypovolemia/).

Put that next to the sex differences above and a picture forms. If women already start with less blood and a smaller stroke volume for their size, they have less margin before standing pushes the heart rate up to compensate. That does not mean POTS is simply being a woman. Most women never get it, and up to half of people with POTS can point to a trigger such as an infection, surgery or pregnancy ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024)). But it helps explain why the same trigger may tip more women than men over the line.

## How do hormones and your cycle come into it?

Estrogen and progesterone affect how much salt and water your kidneys hold on to, and that changes across the month. In a study of 10 women with POTS and 11 healthy women, each person stood for 2 hours in two phases of their cycle: the early follicular phase (just after your period starts, when both hormones are low) and the mid-luteal phase (about a week after ovulation, when both are high) ([Fu et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1161/HYPERTENSIONAHA.110.151787)). In the women with POTS, the early follicular phase brought lower stroke volume after 30 minutes of standing and lower levels of renin and aldosterone, the hormones that tell the body to keep salt and water. Near-fainting was more common in the early follicular phase in both groups. The authors suggest the higher hormone levels of the luteal phase help the body retain more fluid, which helped them cope better later in the 2 hours of standing.

A second study from the same group found the cycle did not change the nerve signals that tighten blood vessels in women with POTS, which suggests fluid and blood vessel changes, rather than the nervous system itself, drive the monthly swing ([Stickford et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP270088)). And when women were asked about their own experience, both those with POTS and healthy women felt most lightheaded during their period and least in the follicular phase that follows, with the POTS group more lightheaded at every phase ([Peggs et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijgo.2012.04.014)). The same study found women with POTS reported more estrogen-related gynecologic conditions.

Big hormonal turning points show up in the story too. About half of people first get symptoms as teenagers, most often around 14 ([Shaw et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.12895)), and pregnancy is one of the most common triggers. If that is where you are, see [POTS and pregnancy](/insights/pots/pots-and-pregnancy/) and [menopause, perimenopause and HRV](/insights/hrv/menopause-hrv-dysautonomia/).

<div class="callout callout-note"><strong>Your cycle can explain a pattern, not every bad day.</strong> The studies above are small, and every body is different. A flare around your period is common, but a flare can also come from heat, a virus, poor sleep or overdoing it.</div>

## Are men with POTS being missed?

It is a fair question, since a condition seen as a "young women's" condition might be overlooked in men. The evidence so far does not show that. In the survey of 8,919 people, men and women were misdiagnosed at similar rates (76.2% and 74.9%) and saw a similar number of doctors before diagnosis, a median of 5 each ([Bourne et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjco.2021.08.014)). The difference ran the other way: women waited a median of 1.5 years for a diagnosis, men about 11 months. The authors concluded that women face more challenges getting diagnosed.

So the gap is not mostly men going undiagnosed. If anything, it suggests women's symptoms take longer to be taken seriously. If you are still looking for answers, [how POTS is diagnosed](/insights/pots/how-pots-is-diagnosed/) explains what the test involves, so you can ask for it by name.

## What can you do with this?

Knowing why POTS skews female does not change the basic treatment, but it gives you some useful angles.

- **Track your symptoms against your cycle.** If your worst weeks keep landing around your period, that is useful for you and your doctor. [The menstrual cycle and HRV](/insights/hrv/menstrual-cycle-hrv-pots/) explains how your heart rate and HRV shift across the month.
- **Measure, then compare like with like.** A stand test at the same phase of your cycle each time stops a normal monthly swing from looking like a setback. See [the orthostatic stand test at home](/insights/pots/the-orthostatic-stand-test-at-home/).
- **Ask about the volume side.** Since low blood volume is part of the picture, ask your doctor whether the fluid and salt steps in the guidelines suit you, and whether they want you to adjust around your period.
- **Know that the heart can adapt.** In the study of 27 people with POTS, a specially designed 3-month exercise program grew the heart's main pumping chamber by about 12% and blood volume by about 7%, lowered upright heart rate by 9 beats per minute, and 10 of the 19 who finished no longer met the criteria for POTS ([Fu et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2010.02.043)). If you get crashes a day or two after effort, talk to your doctor before starting, because that changes the advice.

## The bottom line

POTS is far more common in women, with about nine in ten patients female and most diagnosed between their teens and forties. There is no single proven reason, but the research points to several things stacking up: lower blood volume and stroke volume for body size, less blood returning to the heart when upright, and a cycle that changes how much fluid the body holds on to, with symptoms often worst around the period. Women also wait longer for a diagnosis. Tracking your symptoms against your cycle can show whether your hard days follow a pattern you can plan around.
