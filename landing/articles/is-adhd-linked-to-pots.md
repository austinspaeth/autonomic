If you have POTS, you probably know the feeling: you read the same paragraph three times, lose your train of thought mid-sentence and forget why you walked into a room. Many people with POTS, especially young women, start to wonder whether it is actually ADHD. **ADHD and POTS do seem to overlap, partly through joint hypermobility, but POTS itself also causes attention problems that look a lot like ADHD, so the two are easy to mix up.** Here is what the studies show, how to tell them apart and what to know about ADHD medicines if you have POTS.

## What does the research say about ADHD and POTS?

The evidence comes from a handful of small studies, but they point the same way. In a Vanderbilt study, 21 people with POTS were compared with 18 people with ADHD and 20 healthy controls. The people with POTS scored significantly higher than controls on inattention and ADHD scales. The key detail: **those symptoms had not been present in childhood** ([Raj et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp.2008.144360)). Because ADHD starts in childhood, that suggests much of the inattention in POTS comes from POTS itself rather than undiagnosed lifelong ADHD. The same study found people with POTS did not have higher rates of anxiety disorders than the general population, despite often being told it was anxiety.

Researchers who treat POTS also see the overlap in the clinic. A Vanderbilt drug trial described ADHD as one of the conditions people with POTS commonly have alongside it ([Green et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.113.000395)). What is missing is a large study that measures how many people with POTS have formally diagnosed ADHD, so be wary of exact percentages you see quoted online.

## Why might hypermobility link them?

The clearest thread connecting the two is joint hypermobility. In a UK study of 109 adults with a formal neurodevelopmental diagnosis, including ADHD, autism and Tourette syndrome, 51% were hypermobile, compared with about 20% of the general population. They also reported significantly more symptoms of orthostatic intolerance and pain than a comparison group, and the number of hypermobile joints helped explain that link ([Csecs et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2021.786916)). The odds of hypermobility were higher in women than men.

Hypermobility matters for attention in its own right. In a 2026 pilot study, people with hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (hEDS), many of whom have POTS, had worse attention than healthy controls, and their executive function was worse while standing than while lying down ([Müller et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10286-026-01191-7)). Researchers don't yet know exactly why these conditions cluster. For more on the hypermobility side, see [hypermobility, EDS and POTS](/insights/pots/hypermobility-eds-and-pots/).

## Is it POTS brain fog or ADHD?

Brain fog is close to universal in POTS. In a survey of 138 young people with POTS, 132 reported it, and they described it as being forgetful, cloudy and having trouble focusing, thinking and communicating ([Ross et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10286-013-0212-z)). From the inside, that can feel a lot like ADHD. What sets brain fog apart is what makes it worse.

No single sign settles it, but these differences are a useful starting point:

| Clue | More like POTS brain fog | More like ADHD |
|---|---|---|
| When it started | With or after other POTS symptoms | In childhood, before POTS |
| Day to day | Comes and goes with bad days and flares | Fairly steady across days |
| Posture | Worse upright, can ease lying down | Not tied to posture |
| Triggers | Poor sleep, dehydration, heat, standing, fatigue | Shows up at home, school and work alike |
| Other signs | Racing heart, lightheadedness, fatigue alongside it | Restlessness or impulsivity, often lifelong |

The catch is that you can have both. Someone with lifelong ADHD who then develops POTS may find their focus gets much worse on POTS days. Ross and colleagues also found that brain fog often lingered after lying down, so posture alone does not rule POTS out. For more on the brain side of POTS, see [long COVID brain fog](/insights/postviral/long-covid-brain-fog/), which covers the same mechanisms.

<div class="callout callout-tip"><strong>Track it for two weeks.</strong> Rate your focus each day from 0 to 10, and note your sleep, fluids, how long you stood and your heart rate. If your worst focus days line up with poor sleep and long upright days, POTS is likely doing a lot of the work. If focus stays poor whatever your POTS is doing, mention that to your doctor.</div>

## How do ADHD medicines affect POTS?

This is where it matters most to get the full picture. ADHD medicines can raise heart rate, and the Canadian POTS guidance lists stimulants, including ADHD medications, among the medicines that can push heart rate up ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024)). In a randomised trial in 27 people with POTS, a single 40 mg dose of atomoxetine, a non-stimulant ADHD medicine that blocks noradrenaline reuptake, raised average standing heart rate from 105 to 121 beats per minute compared with placebo and made symptoms worse ([Green et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.113.000395)).

But stimulants are not simply off the table. Some POTS specialists use them for fatigue and brain fog:

- In 24 adults with hard-to-treat POTS given methylphenidate, 14 of the 18 who completed six months of follow-up reported marked improvement in fatigue and near-fainting ([Kanjwal et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1097/MJT.0b013e3181dd21d2)).
- In a children's hospital POTS program, 517 patients were treated for fatigue or brain fog. Methylphenidate helped 53% of those who tried it, while atomoxetine helped only 17% ([Boris and Bernadzikowski, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1047951118001415)).
- In the brain fog survey, stimulant medications were among the treatments people with POTS most often reported using ([Ross et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10286-013-0212-z)).

None of these were controlled trials of stimulants, so they show that some people do well, not that stimulants work for everyone. The practical point is that ADHD medicine and POTS can interact in either direction, which is why any start, stop or dose change belongs with the doctor who prescribes it. If you already take an ADHD medicine, do not stop it on your own; tell your POTS doctor you take it and ask whether it could be raising your heart rate.

## What should you ask your doctor?

Bring a short list, ideally with a couple of weeks of notes:

1. Could my focus problems be POTS brain fog, ADHD or both? Is an ADHD assessment worthwhile, and who does them?
2. If I take an ADHD medicine, could it be raising my heart rate? Should we check a stand test on and off it, rather than me stopping it alone?
3. Should I be checked for joint hypermobility or hEDS?
4. Are there POTS treatments that might also help the brain fog, such as fluids, compression or exercise?
5. If fog is the symptom that limits me most, what are the options?

A record of when your focus was worst, next to your sleep, fluids and heart rate, gives your doctor far more to go on than "I can't concentrate". [Turning your data into a doctor conversation](/insights/recovery/turn-your-data-into-a-doctor-conversation/) covers how to fit it on one page. If you have been told your POTS symptoms are just anxiety, [is it POTS or anxiety](/insights/pots/is-it-pots-or-anxiety/) may help too.

## The bottom line

ADHD and POTS do overlap, and joint hypermobility seems to be part of the reason. But POTS itself causes inattention and brain fog that can look just like ADHD, and in one study those problems had not been there in childhood. Brain fog tends to follow sleep, fluids, standing and fatigue, while ADHD is lifelong and steadier. ADHD medicines can raise heart rate in POTS, yet some stimulants are used to treat POTS fatigue, so the answer is personal. Track your focus alongside your POTS for a couple of weeks and take it to your doctor.
