When someone you love has POTS, it can be hard to know what to do. They look fine, then suddenly need to sit down. They were out yesterday and can barely get up today. You want to help, but you are not sure whether to step in, back off or ask. **The best way to help someone with POTS is to believe them, take on the practical things that make it worse, like heat, standing and rushing, and ask them what helps rather than guessing.** Here is a plain guide for partners, parents and friends.

## What does POTS feel like from the inside?

POTS (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome) means the heart rate rises too far on standing, with symptoms such as lightheadedness, palpitations and shakiness that ease when lying down. In adults the threshold is a sustained rise of at least 30 beats per minute within 10 minutes of standing, and symptoms have to have lasted at least 3 months ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024); [Sheldon et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hrthm.2015.03.029)).

That clinical definition hides how much it affects a day. In the largest survey of people with POTS, almost everyone reported lightheadedness, a racing heart and feeling close to fainting, and most also had headaches and trouble concentrating ([Shaw et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.12895)).

It also shapes a life. In the same survey, 94% were women and about half first had symptoms as teenagers, and the median wait for a diagnosis was 24 months ([Shaw et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.12895)). In a separate survey of 5,556 adults with POTS, only 48% had worked in the previous three months, two thirds of those said they would work more if not for their illness, and 70.5% had lost income because of it ([Bourne et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.13245)). If someone you love seems to be cancelling plans or cutting back at work, this is usually why.

## What practical help actually makes a difference?

The everyday triggers for POTS are well known, and the guidelines advise avoiding warm environments and long standing ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024)). Those are exactly the places where someone nearby can help without making a fuss.

| Situation | How you can help | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Heat | Choose cool places, offer the shady seat, keep showers and rooms from getting too hot | Heat widens blood vessels and makes pooling worse |
| Standing still | Queue for them, find a seat, cook together so they can sit for part of it | Standing still is one of the hardest things with POTS |
| Getting up | Give them time to sit on the edge of the bed or chair before standing | Getting up quickly is a common trigger |
| Water and salt | Keep water within reach and salty snacks if their doctor has advised more salt | Many treatment plans start with more fluid and salt |
| Plans | Build in places to sit and an easy way to leave early | They can say yes more often if no is always allowed |

The fluid and salt advice is worth a word of care. The Canadian guidance suggests around 3 liters of fluid and 10 grams of salt a day for many people with POTS ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024)), but extra salt is not right for everyone, so follow their doctor's plan rather than adding salt to their food on their behalf. For the reasoning behind these triggers, see [heat intolerance and POTS](/insights/pots/heat-intolerance-and-pots/).

<div class="callout callout-tip"><strong>Ask, don't assume.</strong> What helps one person with POTS can feel smothering to another. A simple "What would make today easier?" lets them tell you, and it is one of the questions Dysautonomia International suggests friends and family ask.</div>

## What should you not say to someone with POTS?

Many people with POTS have spent years being doubted before anyone named it. In a long-term survey of people diagnosed with POTS as children or teens, nearly three quarters had diagnostic delays and over half had been told their symptoms were "in their head" ([Boris et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.123.033485)). By the time it is diagnosed, words that sound harmless can land on an old bruise. Phrases to avoid:

- **"You don't look sick."** POTS is invisible. Looking fine is not the same as feeling fine.
- **"It's probably just anxiety."** A racing heart can feel like panic, but POTS is a physical condition with a measurable heart rate rise on standing.
- **"You could do it yesterday."** POTS changes from day to day. Dysautonomia International's advice for families makes the same point: being able to do something one day does not mean someone can do it the next ([Gefroh, 2026](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/blog/wordpress/how-to-help-someone-you-care-about-who-has-pots/)).
- **"Have you tried just exercising more?"** Exercise is part of many POTS treatment plans, but it has to start slowly and lying down, and for people who also crash after effort the advice is different. See [POTS exercise or pacing: which advice applies](/insights/pots/pots-exercise-or-pacing-which-advice-applies/).
- **"You should try..."** Unless they ask for ideas, most people with POTS have heard plenty. Listening helps more.

What to say instead is usually simpler: "How are you feeling today?", "Do you want to sit?" or "I believe you."

## How can you tell it's a bad day, and when is it urgent?

Every person's bad days look a little different, so ask them what theirs look like and what helps. Common signs are needing to sit or lie down more, being pale or flushed, struggling to follow a conversation, or being quieter than usual. Bad days are often set off by heat, illness, poor sleep, a big meal or doing more the day before. For the usual triggers and how people recover, see [POTS flare-ups: triggers and recovery](/insights/pots/pots-flare-up-triggers-and-recovery/).

If they feel faint, help them sit or lie down straight away, somewhere cool if you can, because POTS symptoms ease when lying down. Counter-pressure moves, such as crossing the legs and squeezing or tensing the thighs, can buy time to get to a seat ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024)). More on those in [counter-pressure maneuvers for POTS](/insights/pots/counterpressure-maneuvers-pots-orthostatic-intolerance/).

Some symptoms are not their usual POTS and need urgent care. Get help straight away for fainting with an injury, fainting while lying down or during exercise, chest pain with effort or that does not settle when they lie down, a racing heart that will not slow at rest, or new weakness, numbness on one side, confusion or trouble speaking.

## How can you help at appointments?

Appointments are short, and it is hard to remember three months of symptoms in ten minutes, especially with brain fog. You can help in a few ways:

1. **Before:** help them write down their main symptoms, what makes them worse, what they have tried and the questions they want answered.
2. **During:** offer to come along and take notes, so they can focus on the conversation. Let them lead and speak for themselves.
3. **After:** help them keep track of any new plan, so you both know what changed and when.

A written record makes the biggest difference. Heart rate from a stand test, a short daily symptom score and notes on what changed give a doctor far more to work with than "it's been bad lately". [Turning your data into a doctor conversation](/insights/recovery/turn-your-data-into-a-doctor-conversation/) covers how to fit it on one page.

## How do you look after yourself?

Supporting someone with a long-term condition is a lot, and you will help them better if you are not running on empty. It is normal to feel worried, frustrated or helpless at times. A few things help: learn about POTS from good sources so you feel less in the dark, share the load with other family or friends, keep some of your own routines, and talk honestly with the person about what you can and cannot do. Dysautonomia International's family guidance also reminds people that being present and listening often matters more than fixing anything ([Gefroh, 2026](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/blog/wordpress/how-to-help-someone-you-care-about-who-has-pots/)).

<div class="callout callout-note"><strong>Keep inviting them.</strong> People with POTS often stop being asked because they say no so often. An invitation with an easy way out, like "come if you're up to it, there's a seat by the door", tells them they still belong.</div>

## The bottom line

POTS is real, common in young women and invisible from the outside, and many people with it have waited years to be believed. The most useful things you can do are practical: keep them out of heat and long standing, make it easy to sit, keep water nearby and give them time to get up. Avoid comparing today with yesterday or suggesting it is anxiety, learn what a bad day looks like and which symptoms need urgent care, and offer to help them bring a clear record to appointments. When in doubt, ask them what would help.

<div class="callout callout-warn"><strong>Not medical advice.</strong> This article is educational and does not replace care from a clinician who knows the person's history. Talk to their doctor before changing salt or fluid intake, activity, treatment or medication. If symptoms are new, worsening or frightening, get medical care.</div>
