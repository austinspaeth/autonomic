## When a stressful week makes everything harder

If a tough week leaves your heart racing more, your legs heavier, your head foggier and your body generally less willing to stand up, you are reading your own system correctly. **Stress does make POTS worse.** A stressful day adds a surge of sympathetic "fight or flight" activity, with a dose of adrenaline and noradrenaline, on top of an autonomic nervous system that already struggles to balance the ordinary act of standing. Symptoms flare, and your heart rate variability tends to drop for a while.

There is an important second half to that answer, and it is the part that actually helps: stress is an **amplifier**, not the **cause**. POTS is a physical condition, and the racing heart that defines it is a mechanical response to gravity, not a panic attack. Once you separate "stress flares my POTS" from "stress gave me POTS," the things that calm a flare stop sounding like a brush-off and start sounding like what they are: ways to take load off a real system.

## How stress lands on a system that is already stretched

Your autonomic nervous system runs on two branches that normally balance each other: the sympathetic "fight or flight" branch that speeds things up, and the parasympathetic, or vagal, branch that calms them down. Standing up is a small autonomic challenge for everyone. Gravity pulls blood toward your legs and belly, and a healthy system answers by tightening blood vessels and nudging the heart, so flow to the brain holds steady. In POTS, that compensation falls short, and the heart overshoots to make up for it, which is why standing produces a sustained rise of at least 30 bpm in adults ([Sheldon et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hrthm.2015.03.029)).

Now add stress. Psychological stress switches on that same sympathetic branch and releases adrenaline and noradrenaline. In a body that is already leaning hard on sympathetic activity just to keep upright, a fresh stress response has less headroom and lands harder. This is why a stressful week often shows up as a faster resting heart, a bigger jump on standing, more dizziness and more fatigue, and why clinicians list emotional and physical stress alongside heat, dehydration, illness, poor sleep and hormonal shifts as common triggers for a [POTS flare](/insights/pots/pots-flare-up-triggers-and-recovery/).

In some people the sympathetic side runs especially hot. In the hyperadrenergic pattern of POTS, standing drives large adrenaline surges that can feel like tremor, chest pounding and a wave of dread, which stress makes more likely to tip over. That overlap is exactly why these surges are so easy to mistake for panic, a question worth untangling on its own in [is it POTS or anxiety](/insights/pots/is-it-pots-or-anxiety/).

## What stress does to your HRV

Heart rate variability is the small, beat-to-beat variation in the gaps between heartbeats, and it is a direct read on that sympathetic and parasympathetic push and pull. When the calming vagal branch is in charge, the gaps vary more and HRV is higher. When the sympathetic branch takes over, the gaps even out and HRV falls.

A meta-analysis of stress studies found that psychological stress consistently lowers the vagally driven, high-frequency part of HRV and shifts the balance toward sympathetic activity ([Kim et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.30773/pi.2017.08.17)). So a dip in your HRV on a stressful day is the expected reading, not evidence that something new has broken. That study was in general populations rather than POTS specifically, but the mechanism is the same one POTS leans on, which is why stress and a POTS flare often show up together in the numbers.

The practical takeaway is to read HRV as a **trend**, not a verdict. One low morning after a hard day tells you little. A run of low mornings, read beside your resting heart rate and how you actually feel, tells you the system is carrying load and could use recovery. Slow, paced breathing is one of the few things that reliably nudges HRV back up in the moment, which is part of why it helps here (more on that below, and in [resonant breathing](/insights/hrv/resonant-breathing-hrv-biofeedback/)).

## But stress is not the cause of POTS

This is the claim that gets POTS misunderstood, so it is worth being precise. If anxiety or stress were the engine behind the racing heart, then a stressful mental task should spike the heart rate of a person with POTS far more than a healthy person's. It does not.

In a study from the Mayo Clinic, researchers measured the heart-rate response to a demanding mental task (a Stroop colour-word test) in people with POTS and in healthy controls. The rise was about the same in both groups, roughly 18 versus 19 bpm, and it was not related to anxiety scores. Anxiety, somatic vigilance and catastrophic thoughts about symptoms were higher in the POTS group, but those scores did not explain the heart-rate response ([Masuki et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00927.2006)). In other words, the thing that makes POTS POTS, the excessive rise on **standing**, is a physical, posture-driven response, not a product of being anxious.

Larger studies back this up at the level of diagnosis. People with POTS do **not** have a higher lifetime or current rate of clinical anxiety disorders than the general population, even though questionnaires that mix physical and emotional symptoms can make them look more anxious because so many POTS symptoms (palpitations, breathlessness, shakiness) are physical ([Raj et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp.2008.144360); [Owens et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.autneu.2018.03.004)). Being told your condition is "just stress" is both common and, as a root-cause claim, wrong.

## The symptom and fear feedback loop

None of that means the mind plays no part. It means the part it plays is a **loop**, not a cause. POTS symptoms are genuinely frightening: a heart that pounds without warning, in public or at 3 a.m., teaches the brain to watch for the next episode. Studies find that people with POTS score higher on measures of somatic vigilance and catastrophic thoughts about bodily sensations, essentially a heightened alarm about what the heart is doing ([Owens et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.autneu.2018.03.004)).

That alarm is itself a stressor, and it feeds the same sympathetic branch that is already overworked. Palpitations raise worry, worry raises sympathetic drive, sympathetic drive makes the next flare more likely. The loop is real, and it is also the most workable part of the whole picture, because you can interrupt it without needing to fix the underlying autonomic wiring.

## What actually calms a stress flare

Because stress is an amplifier, the useful moves are the ones that lower sympathetic load, both in the moment and over the days that follow. None of this treats POTS itself, and none of it replaces your clinician's plan, but it is how most people take the edge off a stress-driven flare.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Slow paced breathing, about 5 to 6 breaths a minute | Shifts the balance toward the calming vagal branch and nudges HRV up in the moment | During a flare, and as a daily habit |
| Sit or lie down, legs up | Removes the orthostatic load stacked on top of the stress | The moment symptoms spike |
| Fluids with electrolytes, and salt if advised | Supports blood volume, a core part of POTS management | Through the day, more on hard days |
| Get out of heat | Heat widens vessels and adds to blood pooling, compounding the flare | Any warm environment |
| Protect sleep | Poor sleep is its own sympathetic stressor and a common flare trigger | The nights around a stressful stretch |
| Ease activity, do not push through | Prevents stacking exertion on an already loaded system | While the flare settles |

A few of these have their own deeper guides worth reading when you are not mid-flare: slow breathing and the other levers in [how to stimulate the vagus nerve](/insights/hrv/how-to-stimulate-the-vagus-nerve/), in-the-moment tactics in [how to lower a high heart rate](/insights/pots/how-to-lower-high-heart-rate-pots/), and the broader habit of spotting what sets you off in [finding your triggers](/insights/recovery/find-your-triggers-symptom-journal/).

## When to get help, and what not to change on your own

Treating stress as a lever does not mean toughing out everything alone. Get medical care if symptoms are new, severe or frightening: fainting, chest pain, a heart rate that will not settle, or anything that feels different from your usual flares. A flare that drags on for weeks, or a steadily rising resting heart rate, is also worth a clinician's eyes rather than a tracker's.

And do not start, stop or change any medication because of a stressful stretch or a tracker reading. Several POTS medications act directly on heart rate and blood pressure, and adjusting them around a stressful week is exactly the kind of change that belongs in a conversation with the person who prescribed them. Bringing a record of which days flared you, and what your heart rate and HRV did, makes that conversation far more useful than "I have been stressed and I feel awful."

## The bottom line

Does stress make POTS worse? Yes. Stress adds a sympathetic "fight or flight" surge, with adrenaline, onto a nervous system that already struggles to balance standing, so symptoms flare and HRV tends to fall. But stress is an amplifier, not the cause: the posture-driven racing heart that defines POTS is physical, about the same under mental stress whether or not you have POTS, and not a sign of an anxiety disorder you do not have. That distinction is good news, because it means the ordinary tools, slow breathing, pacing, sleep, fluids and salt, are real ways to take load off a real system. You cannot always control the stressor. You can lower what it stacks on top of.
