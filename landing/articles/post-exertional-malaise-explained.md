If you have long COVID or ME/CFS, you may know this pattern: you do something ordinary, a walk, a long phone call, a family event, and feel more or less fine. Then a day or two later the floor drops out. **Post-exertional malaise (PEM)** is the name for that delayed, outsized worsening of symptoms after effort. In plain terms, it is what people mean when they say they "crashed," and recognising it is the first step to having fewer crashes.

This page covers what PEM is, the symptoms, how long PEM lasts, what counts as exertion, what the research shows about its biology, and what to do in a crash.

## What is post-exertional malaise?

The CDC describes PEM as the worsening of symptoms after even minor physical or mental exertion that would have been tolerated before, and calls it a hallmark of ME/CFS ([CDC, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)). NICE defines it as a worsening of symptoms that can follow minimal cognitive, physical, emotional or social activity ([NICE, 2021](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206)).

Three features set it apart from ordinary tiredness:

- **It is out of proportion.** A task that used to cost nothing now costs days.
- **It is often delayed.** The worst of it frequently arrives the next day or the day after.
- **It is whole-body.** Fatigue is joined by brain fog, pain, poor sleep and flu-like feelings.

PEM is required for an ME/CFS diagnosis, and it is very common in long COVID. In an international survey of 3,762 people with long COVID, it was among the three most frequent symptoms after month six, alongside fatigue and cognitive problems ([Davis et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2021.101019)). How far the two conditions overlap is covered in [is long COVID the same as ME/CFS?](/insights/mecfs/is-long-covid-the-same-as-me-cfs/)

## PEM symptoms: what a crash feels like

People describe PEM in many ways: an "absolute crash," feeling "knocked out," limbs like cement ([Vøllestad and Mengshoel, 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2023.1257557)). In the largest detailed survey, 150 people with ME/CFS listed what flared after exertion. Fatigue was reported by 94 percent, and cognitive difficulties, sleep problems, headaches, muscle pain and flu-like feelings were each reported by more than 30 percent ([Chu et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0197811)).

| Area | What people report |
| --- | --- |
| **Energy** | Heavy, drained, "poisoned" fatigue that rest does not clear quickly; weakness |
| **Cognitive** | Brain fog, slower thinking, trouble finding words, mentally tired after small tasks |
| **Pain** | Muscle pain, joint pain, headaches |
| **Flu-like** | Feeling ill or feverish, as if coming down with something |
| **Sleep** | Unrefreshing or broken sleep, waking as tired as you went to bed |
| **Orthostatic** | Worse dizziness, racing heart or lightheadedness on standing |
| **Other** | Gut symptoms, low mood or irritability, other nervous-system symptoms |

Not everyone gets all of these, and the mix can change from one crash to the next. Use the table as a way to notice the pattern, not to score yourself.

## How long does PEM last?

The short answer from the guidelines: symptoms typically worsen 12 to 48 hours after activity and can last for days or even weeks ([CDC, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/healthcare-providers/clinical-care-patients-mecfs/treating-most-disruptive-symptoms.html); [NICE, 2021](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206)). Patient surveys show how wide that range is in real life.

| Finding | Onset | Duration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Chu et al., 2018** (150 ME/CFS patients) | 42% said onset varies; up to 37% said it sometimes starts a day or more later | 84% said it can last 24 hours or more; 24% said the longest episodes go past a week |
| **Hartle et al., 2021**, as summarised by Vøllestad and Mengshoel | Minutes 35%, hours 40%, a day or more 25% | Several days 48%; a week or more 13% |
| **CDC and NICE guidance** | Typically 12 to 48 hours after activity | Days or even weeks |

Two things follow. First, PEM is not always delayed: many people feel some of it straight away, and the next-day hit comes on top. Second, a crash that lasts a week is not unusual, so a two-day rest after a big effort is not a guarantee.

### PEM versus ordinary fatigue

Not every tired day is PEM. Ordinary fatigue matches the effort, shows up soon and lifts with a night's sleep. PEM does not. This is a rough guide, not a diagnosis.

| | Ordinary fatigue | Post-exertional malaise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Timing** | During or right after effort | Often delayed, typically 12 to 48 hours |
| **Proportion** | Matches the effort | Out of proportion to the effort |
| **What flares** | Mostly energy and sleepiness | Whole body: cognition, pain, sleep, orthostatic symptoms |
| **Recovery** | A night's sleep | Days to weeks; sleep is unrefreshing |
| **Response to rest** | Optional | Essential and protective |

## What counts as exertion?

"Post-exertional" makes it sound like exercise. It is much broader than that. In the studies, the triggers people report include ([Chu et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0197811); [Vøllestad and Mengshoel, 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2023.1257557)):

- **Physical:** walking, chores, showering, carrying things, climbing stairs. Medium physical activity was the most common trigger in one survey.
- **Cognitive:** reading, screen work, meetings, paperwork, planning.
- **Emotional:** stress, conflict, upsetting news. In the Chu survey, 90 percent said both exertion and emotional distress could bring on PEM.
- **Orthostatic:** standing in a queue, cooking at the stove, changes in position. Upright time is real load if you have POTS; see [orthostatic load and upright time](/insights/pots/orthostatic-load-upright-time-pots/).
- **Sensory and social:** noise, sensory overload, a busy shop, an outing with friends.

These add up across a day. A short walk on a day that also held a hard conversation and two hours of screen time is not the same short walk on a quiet day. That stacking is why [pacing and the energy envelope](/insights/recovery/pacing-101-energy-envelope/) count all kinds of effort, not just steps.

## Is PEM physical? What the evidence shows

PEM is not "just in your head," and it is not simply being out of shape. The evidence is still young and the studies are small, but several lines point to a measurable change in how the body handles and recovers from effort.

### Two-day exercise tests in ME/CFS

In a two-day cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET), a person does a maximal cycling test, then repeats it 24 hours later. Healthy people, even unfit ones, usually do about as well on day two. Many people with ME/CFS do worse.

A 2020 meta-analysis pooled five studies (98 patients, 51 controls). The clearest difference was workload at the ventilatory threshold, roughly the point where the body leans harder on less efficient energy pathways: it fell by 16.6 watts in patients and rose by 3.9 watts in controls ([Lim et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm9124040)). A 2024 study of 84 ME/CFS patients and 71 sedentary controls found day-two declines in work, oxygen use and heart rate at peak exertion in patients, and the differences held in pairs matched for fitness ([Keller et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-024-05410-5)).

The picture is not clean. The meta-analysis covered only 149 people, used mixed diagnostic criteria, and its authors call for more trials before using the test for diagnosis. A fitness-matched study found most of the differences disappeared, apart from a higher sense of effort ([Vøllestad and Mengshoel, 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2023.1257557)). The test also needs people well enough to complete it, which leaves out the most severely ill. It is a research tool and a specialist option, not a routine test.

### Muscle changes after PEM in long COVID

A 2024 study in *Nature Communications* took thigh muscle biopsies from 25 people with long COVID and 21 people who had fully recovered from COVID, before and one day after a maximal cycling test ([Appelman et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-44432-3)). The long COVID group had lower exercise capacity and a higher share of easily fatigued muscle fibres. After the test, a mitochondrial enzyme fell in the patients only, and the authors describe exercise-induced muscle damage. Amyloid-containing deposits in muscle were higher in patients from the start and rose after exercise in both groups.

It was also useful for what it did not find. The authors argue deconditioning does not explain the results (the patients averaged around 4,000 steps a day), saw no capillary blockage, and found that viral protein in muscle did not differ between groups. It is one small study of severe long COVID and needs repeating. For the wider theories, see [what causes long COVID](/insights/postviral/what-causes-long-covid/), and for how this differs from being out of shape, [POTS vs deconditioning](/insights/pots/pots-vs-deconditioning/).

## Why "push through" advice can backfire

Graded exercise therapy builds up activity in fixed steps on the idea that the body will adapt. With PEM, each step can become a fresh trigger instead. NICE's 2021 ME/CFS guideline recommends against offering any programme that uses fixed incremental increases in physical activity or exercise, such as graded exercise therapy ([NICE, 2021](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206)). The CDC warns that standard exercise advice for healthy people can substantially harm people with ME/CFS, and that the "push and crash" cycle of doing too much on good days can lead to relapse ([CDC, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/healthcare-providers/clinical-care-patients-mecfs/treating-most-disruptive-symptoms.html)).

That does not mean all movement is off limits. Gentle, symptom-led activity inside your limits can be part of care. POTS reconditioning such as the [Levine protocol](/insights/pots/exercise-for-pots-levine-protocol/) helps some people, but if you have PEM it should only be tried with a clinician who treats PEM as the ceiling.

## What to do in a PEM crash

There is no proven treatment that ends a crash, so the aim is to stop adding load and let it pass. Agree the details with your clinician, but the common ground in the guidance looks like this:

1. **Rest early and fully.** Lie down if that is easier on you than sitting, and cancel what you can.
2. **Cut the hidden load.** Screens, phone calls, decisions and noise all cost energy. Dim the room and keep conversations short. The CDC advises planning rest around mental effort, not just physical effort.
3. **Reduce upright time.** Shower seated or skip it, sit to prepare food, keep things within reach. If you have POTS, keep up the fluids and salt your clinician has already advised.
4. **Do not test yourself.** The first better morning is not the end of the crash. Trying to "see if you are over it" is a common way to start the next one.
5. **Return slowly.** Come back to your usual activities in smaller pieces than before. The CDC suggests breaking tasks into shorter, less strenuous parts and increasing activity cautiously while watching for setbacks.
6. **Write it down.** Note what happened in the two days before. Over time this shows your own triggers.

NICE separates a flare-up, which usually settles within a few days with temporary changes in energy management, from a relapse, which needs a larger, lasting change ([NICE, 2021](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206)). If a crash is not lifting, or you have new or frightening symptoms, contact your clinician.

## Questions clinicians ask to screen for PEM

There is no blood test for PEM. Clinicians ask about it. The CDC suggests questions like these, and a symptom and activity diary for a couple of weeks ([CDC, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/diagnosis/index.html)):

- What happens when you try to push to do activities that are now hard for you?
- How much activity, physical or mental, does it take for you to feel ill?
- How long does it take for you to feel better after that exertion?
- What activities do you now avoid because of what happens after?
- How do you feel after sleeping or resting?

Researchers at DePaul University built a short questionnaire, the DSQ-PEM, to screen for PEM. Its five core items ask how often and how badly, over the past six months, you have had a dead, heavy feeling after starting to exercise; next-day soreness or fatigue after everyday activities; mental tiredness after the slightest effort; physical tiredness after minimal exercise; and feeling drained or sick after mild activity. Further items ask how long you feel worse afterwards. In the validation study, how long symptoms lasted was the strongest single item ([Cotler et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/diagnostics8030066)).

Treat these as a way to describe your experience clearly at an appointment, not as a self-diagnosis. The questionnaire was tested against a limited set of other illnesses, and its authors call for more work.

## My own crashes

Two years ago I was crashing almost daily. My HRV was in the dumpster, and ordinary things set me off: carrying laundry or going up the stairs would send my heart rate through the roof, and I struggled to get through Zoom meetings because the stress of work could tip me into a crash. When I started tracking, I could watch stacked stressors, a bad meal, a short night, a hard day, drag my numbers down, and then watch my actual day fall apart the same way.

Today a crash might happen once or twice every couple of weeks, and when one does I can usually tell you exactly why. They have not gone away. Last year we took our kids to Disney World and I paid for it with a hard crash by the end, but we did the whole trip. I wrote the full story, with the data, in [from daily crashes to living again](/insights/postviral/from-daily-crashes-to-living-again/).

## Seeing a crash before it lands

Because PEM often arrives late, your body can be under strain while you still feel fine. Resting heart rate and HRV sometimes shift in that window, though not for everyone and not every time. What the research shows before, during and after a crash is in [HRV and PEM](/insights/hrv/hrv-and-pem-crash/), and the [pacing budget](/insights/app/pacing-budget-know-how-much-today-can-take/) is how Autonomic turns those readings into a daily allowance of effort.

## The bottom line

Post-exertional malaise is a worsening of symptoms after physical, mental, emotional or orthostatic effort that is out of proportion to what you did, often arrives 12 to 48 hours later, and can last days or weeks. It is the hallmark of ME/CFS and common in long COVID. Two-day exercise tests and a 2024 muscle study point to real physiological changes, though the studies are small. Pushing through tends to make it worse. Resting early, cutting hidden load, returning slowly and pacing inside your limits are how most people get fewer and shorter crashes.
