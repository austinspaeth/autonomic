If you have POTS and long COVID, or POTS and ME/CFS, you have probably been handed two pieces of advice that seem to cancel each other out. One says exercise is one of the best treatments for POTS. The other says pushing yourself is the fastest way to get worse. **If effort makes you crash 12 hours or more later, the pacing advice applies to you first, even though you also have POTS.** The rest of this page explains why, how to check, and how to move safely either way.

## Why does POTS advice say exercise?

Because, for many people with POTS, it works. Levine's group in Dallas found that many people with POTS have smaller hearts and lower blood volume than healthy people of the same age, so the heart has to race to keep blood flowing when they stand. Training can reverse some of that.

The best-known evidence comes from the same study. In it, 25 people with POTS began a three-month program and 19 completed it. Training started semi-recumbent, which the authors called "a critical strategy," with upright exercise added gradually, usually not until the second or third month. After training, 10 of the 19 (53%) no longer met the criteria for POTS, their upright heart rate fell by a median of 9 beats per minute, and quality of life scores improved ([Fu et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2010.02.043)).

That is a real result, and it is why the recumbent-first approach, often called the Levine or Dallas protocol, is standard advice. We cover how it works step by step in [exercise for POTS](/insights/pots/exercise-for-pots-levine-protocol/). It is also worth being clear about its limits: it was a small study, a quarter of the people who started did not finish, and it was not designed for people with post-exertional malaise.

## Why does ME/CFS and long COVID advice say pace?

Because in post-exertional malaise (PEM), effort beyond your limit makes the illness worse, often a day or two later. You do not adapt to the load the way a healthy body does; you pay for it. Programs that increase activity on a fixed schedule, whatever your symptoms are doing, were the core of graded exercise therapy, and the UK's NICE guideline says not to offer people with ME/CFS any program that uses fixed incremental increases in physical activity or exercise ([NICE NG206](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206)).

Long COVID guidance says the same thing. World Physiotherapy's briefing on safe rehabilitation states that "graded exercise therapy or fixed activity prescription should not be used" for people with post-exertional symptom exacerbation, and that quota-based programs "can result in harms" for them ([World Physiotherapy, 2021](https://world.physio/sites/default/files/2021-07/Briefing-Paper-9-Long-Covid-FINAL-English-202107.pdf)). It also cites a survey of 3,762 people with long COVID in 56 countries in which 72% reported post-exertional symptom exacerbation. So this is not a rare exception in long COVID. It may be the majority.

Here is how the two approaches differ in practice.

| | POTS reconditioning | ME/CFS and long COVID pacing |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Rebuild heart size, blood volume and tolerance to standing | Stop crashing, then let capacity recover |
| Starting point | Short recumbent sessions, a few times a week | Find your energy envelope and live inside it |
| How it progresses | On a planned schedule over about three months | Only after a stretch of stable days, and down as well as up |
| Main warning sign | Symptoms during or straight after a session | A delayed crash 12 hours or more later |

## So which one applies to you?

The deciding question is not your diagnosis. It is whether you get post-exertional malaise. POTS and PEM often travel together, especially after COVID, and the label on your chart does not tell you which you have. Someone with POTS and no PEM can usually recondition. Someone with POTS and PEM needs to pace first, and any exercise has to fit inside that.

## How can you screen yourself for PEM?

Researchers at DePaul University developed a short PEM screen from their longer symptom questionnaire, and World Physiotherapy recommends it for long COVID. It asks how often, and how severely, over the past six months you have had each of these ([Cotler et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/diagnostics8030066)):

1. A dead, heavy feeling after starting to exercise
2. Next-day soreness or fatigue after non-strenuous, everyday activities
3. Feeling mentally tired after the slightest effort
4. Minimum exercise making you physically tired
5. Feeling physically drained or sick after mild activity

Each item is rated for frequency and severity. Having any one of them about half the time or more, at moderate severity or worse, is the screen's sign of PEM. The fuller questionnaire also asks how long the worsening lasts, and a worsening that lasts more than 14 hours is part of what points toward ME/CFS.

Two practical tests help too. First, think back to a day you did more than usual: did you feel worse that evening, or did it land the next day or the day after? A delayed crash is the signature. Second, if you keep a log, look at what came two days before your worst days. Our guide to [post-exertional malaise](/insights/postviral/post-exertional-malaise-explained/) goes through the pattern in detail, and [is long COVID the same as ME/CFS](/insights/mecfs/is-long-covid-the-same-as-me-cfs/) covers how often the two overlap.

<div class="callout callout-note">
  <strong>A screen is not a diagnosis.</strong> It tells you whether PEM is likely enough to change how you approach activity. A clinician who knows ME/CFS and long COVID can tell you more, and should be part of any decision to start a structured exercise program.
</div>

## If you have PEM, can you exercise at all?

Usually some movement is possible, but it looks very different from a reconditioning program, and it comes after your symptoms have settled, not as a way to make them settle. World Physiotherapy suggests "Stop. Rest. Pace" when post-exertional symptoms are present, and for orthostatic symptoms it lists non-upright exercise, isometric exercise and compression as options to consider, with continued monitoring for post-exertional symptoms.

In practice, people who pace well and still move tend to follow a few rules:

- **Pace first.** Get to a stretch of stable days without crashes before adding anything. [Pacing 101](/insights/recovery/pacing-101-energy-envelope/) covers how to find your energy envelope.
- **Stay horizontal.** Lying down removes the orthostatic load, so the same movement costs less. Floor-based leg and core work, gentle stretching or a few minutes on a recumbent bike are typical starting points.
- **Keep under your heart rate line.** Many people use resting heart rate plus 15 as a starting ceiling. Our guide to [what heart rate to stay under when pacing](/insights/mecfs/what-heart-rate-should-i-stay-under-when-pacing/) explains where that comes from, with a calculator.
- **Stop before you feel tired.** Fatigue during a session is a late signal in PEM. The Workwell Foundation suggests avoiding more than about two minutes above your threshold and resting until your heart rate is back within ten beats of resting ([Workwell Foundation, 2024](https://workwellfoundation.org/pacing-with-a-heart-rate-monitor-to-minimize-post-exertional-malaise-pem-in-me-cfs-and-long-covid/)).
- **Increase only when stable, and go back down after a crash.** No schedule. A step up stays only if the next two days are steady.

## If you don't have PEM, how should you start?

Start lying down and start small. The Dallas program began semi-recumbent and only moved upright in the second or third month, which is the whole point: you train the heart without fighting gravity every session. Rowing, recumbent cycling and swimming are the usual starting choices, with leg and core strength work alongside.

Even without PEM, POTS can make a too-hard session feel awful the same day: dizziness, a racing heart, nausea. That is a sign to scale the session down, not to give up. Build duration before intensity, and keep upright exercise as a later goal rather than a starting point. The step-by-step plan is in our guide to [exercise for POTS](/insights/pots/exercise-for-pots-levine-protocol/).

Keep watching for PEM as you go. Some people only discover it once they start doing more. A crash that lands a day or two after a session, rather than during it, means switching to the pacing approach until things settle.

## How do you know if exercise is helping or hurting?

Look at the two days after each session, not the session itself. Helpful exercise tends to show up over weeks as a lower heart rate for the same effort, easier standing and steady or improving mornings. Harmful exercise tends to show up within 48 hours as a crash, a higher resting heart rate or a drop in HRV the next morning.

A simple record makes this visible: what you did, for how long, your heart rate during it, and how you felt over the next two days. If you track HRV, a morning drop after a session is often an early warning; we cover the numbers in [HRV and PEM](/insights/hrv/hrv-and-pem-crash/). If several sessions in a row are followed by worse days, the program is ahead of you, whatever its schedule says.

## The bottom line

POTS advice and pacing advice are not really in conflict. They are written for different people, and **post-exertional malaise is what decides which one is yours**. If effort makes you crash a day or so later, pace first and keep any movement lying down, short and below your heart rate line. If it does not, a recumbent-first reconditioning program has good evidence behind it. Either way, let the two days after each step, not the calendar, decide when to do more.
