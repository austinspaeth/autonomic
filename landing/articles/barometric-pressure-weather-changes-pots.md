## Your body reads the weather, and it is not imagining it

If you have POTS or dysautonomia, you have probably had the experience of feeling a change coming before you checked the forecast: the heavy, hard-to-stand day before a storm, the summer afternoon where standing up turns the room white, the crisp winter morning where you actually feel clear for once. It is easy to dismiss weather sensitivity as coincidence or to feel a little silly about it. You should not. **Weather and POTS** are genuinely linked, because the two jobs the weather stresses hardest, keeping your temperature steady and keeping your blood pressure steady, are both run by the same autonomic nervous system that is already struggling in POTS.

This is educational field notes, not medical advice, and none of it is a reason to fear the forecast. The point is the opposite: once you understand what heat, humidity, pressure, and cold actually do to your system, a weather flare stops being a mystery and becomes something you can see coming and plan around.

## Why weather affects the autonomic nervous system at all

Your autonomic nervous system runs the background machinery that keeps you upright and stable: heart rate, the tightness of your blood vessels, sweating, and how blood is distributed around your body. In POTS and dysautonomia, that machinery is already working with less margin, often on top of a lower-than-ideal blood volume. When the weather changes, it hands that system extra work on two fronts at once.

The first is **thermoregulation**. Holding your core temperature steady is entirely an autonomic task, carried out mainly by opening or closing the vessels in your skin and by sweating. The second is **blood pressure control** through the baroreflex, the fast loop that tightens your vessels and adjusts your heart rate the instant you stand. Weather leans on both. That is why the same conditions a healthy body shrugs off can knock a POTS body sideways: there is simply less spare capacity to absorb the swing.

For the deeper picture of the reflex doing that upright work, see [baroreflex sensitivity and orthostatic intolerance](/insights/pots/baroreflex-sensitivity-orthostatic-intolerance/).

## Heat and humidity: the clearest triggers

Heat is the most reliable weather trigger in POTS, and humidity quietly makes it worse. To shed heat, your body widens the blood vessels in your skin and starts to sweat. Both of those work against an autonomic system trying to keep blood pressure up: widened skin vessels let blood pool toward the surface and the legs, and sweating drains fluid and salt from a blood volume that is often low to begin with. Less blood returns to your heart, so your heart rate climbs to compensate, HRV tends to fall, and standing gets harder.

Humidity is the part people underestimate. Sweat only cools you when it **evaporates**, and humid air is already close to saturated, so it evaporates poorly. Your body responds by sweating even more and widening vessels further, chasing a cooling effect it cannot get. The practical result is that a muggy 78F day can be harder on your numbers than a dry 88F one. Humidity effectively lowers the temperature at which heat starts to cost you.

## Barometric pressure: real for many, but more complicated

This is the one people ask about most and the one with the least tidy answer. Barometric pressure is the weight of the atmosphere pressing on you, and it falls as a storm or low-pressure system moves in. A large share of people with POTS, migraine, and chronic pain report feeling worse in the day or two before bad weather. The honest scientific position is that the evidence for barometric pressure as a direct, standalone cause is mixed, and the effect is smaller and more individual than the heat effect. That is worth knowing so you neither dismiss your own experience nor over-attribute every bad day to the barometer.

There are a few plausible mechanisms, and they probably work together:

- **A front is a package deal.** Falling pressure almost never arrives alone. It travels with temperature swings, rising or falling humidity, and wind. Those companions have clear autonomic effects, so even if pressure itself did nothing, the weather change around it would still land on your system.
- **Fluid shifts.** Small changes in external pressure may subtly change how fluid distributes across tissues and how full your vessels feel, which a system already fighting for blood volume can notice more than a healthy one.
- **Baroreceptor and sinus sensitivity.** The pressure-sensing and air-filled parts of the body, from the baroreceptors that read blood pressure to the sinuses, can be sensitive to pressure change, which may feed into the heavy-headed, pressure-behind-the-eyes feeling many describe.

The takeaway is not to treat the barometer as destiny. It is that if you consistently feel the pre-storm slump, that experience is legitimate and traceable, and a weather app's pressure trend can give you a day of warning.

## Cold weather: it genuinely splits people

Cold does not affect everyone with POTS the same way, and that is not a contradiction, it is real physiology pulling in two directions.

For some people, cold is a relief. Cold makes blood vessels constrict, which pushes blood back toward the core and away from the legs, and that is the exact opposite of the pooling problem POTS creates. Better central blood volume can mean better orthostatic tolerance, which is why a subset of people genuinely feel their sharpest in cold, dry weather.

For others, cold makes things worse. It raises the workload on the heart, aggravates Raynaud's and the cold, painful hands and feet that are common in dysautonomia, and stiffens muscles so the leg-muscle pump that normally helps return blood works less well. Cold weather also comes with dry indoor heating that quietly dehydrates you, and a sudden blast of cold can set off sharp heart rate swings. Which side you land on is personal, and it is one of the clearest examples of why a general rule is useless and your own tracking is everything.

Notice the last row. For a lot of people the trigger is not any single condition but the **change** itself: the first heat wave of the season, the front rolling through, the swing from air conditioning into humidity. A stable extreme is often easier to manage than a fast transition, because a transition is precisely the moment the autonomic system has to scramble.

## How to tell whether weather is really your trigger

Here is the honest problem with weather sensitivity: humans are pattern-seeking. You remember the crash that landed the day a storm came in, and you forget the three storms that did nothing and the flare that arrived on a perfectly clear day. Memory alone will convince you of a link whether or not one exists. The only way to know is to write it down and let a few weeks of data settle the question.

It does not need to be elaborate. Each day, alongside your usual readings, jot a one-word weather note: hot, humid, storm coming, cold snap, clear. Then look at whether your harder days actually cluster on particular conditions.

- **Log the reading and the weather together**, at the same time of day, so you are comparing like with like. Same-time-of-day comparison is the backbone of reading any autonomic trend, weather or not.
- **Give it a few weeks.** One stormy bad day proves nothing. A run of bad days that reliably line up with incoming fronts is a real, personal pattern.
- **Watch the cluster, not the single day.** If falling pressure is genuinely your trigger, it will show up as a tendency across many storms, not a perfect one-to-one match.

## Planning for the hard days instead of dreading them

Once you know your triggers, the forecast becomes a tool rather than a threat. If you have confirmed that heat, humidity, or incoming storms cost you, you can front-load the basics the day before: lift your fluids and salt within whatever range your clinician has set with you, put compression on early, plan a lighter schedule, and have cooling ready if heat is part of the package. If cold is your friend, you can use the clear, crisp days for the things that need your best capacity. None of this is about controlling the weather. It is about not being ambushed by it.

And it bears repeating: a weather flare is temporary. A rough reading on a muggy afternoon or a pressure-drop day is your autonomic nervous system doing extra work, not evidence that months of progress have unraveled. Conditions stabilize, you rehydrate and rest, and your numbers come back. Reading that clearly, without the spiral, is half the battle.

## The bottom line

Weather sensitivity in POTS and dysautonomia is real, and it comes from the same place as the rest of your symptoms: an autonomic nervous system with little margin, asked to regulate temperature and blood pressure through a moving target. Heat and humidity are the strongest and best-understood triggers, working by pooling blood and draining fluid. Falling barometric pressure is real for many people but more individual and less direct, likely because a passing front changes several things at once. Cold genuinely helps some people and hurts others. The way through is not to fear the forecast but to track your own patterns, tell a genuine trigger from a coincidence, and plan the hard days so they cost you less.
