## A symptom you can hear, and can't turn off

Ask people with POTS, dysautonomia, or long COVID about their ears, and a surprising number describe the same thing: a ringing, buzzing, or hissing that was not there before, a feeling of fullness or pressure with hearing that tests normal, or ordinary sounds that suddenly feel too loud. Because it is a sound you cannot escape, **tinnitus** can be more distressing than symptoms that are objectively worse, and the fear often outweighs the sensation.

Here is the reassuring headline. In dysautonomia and long COVID, the everyday ear symptoms are usually a **volume** story, not a damage story. Much of the time the sound was always there in the background, and your nervous system has simply turned up the gain on it. That framing matters, because it points you at the same levers that help the rest of your autonomic picture, and it tells you which few ear symptoms belong at a clinic instead.

These are educational field notes, not medical advice. Ear symptoms have a long list of possible causes, and the goal here is to help you describe yours clearly, track them, and recognize the handful that deserve a proper look rather than being filed under dysautonomia.

## Why the nervous system turns the volume up

Tinnitus feels like it comes from the ear, but most of it is generated higher up, in the brain's hearing pathways. The modern understanding is that tinnitus is usually a **central gain** problem. When the auditory system receives slightly less input, or slightly noisier input, the brain compensates by amplifying what it does get, the way an old radio hisses louder when you turn up a weak station. That amplified background activity is what you perceive as a tone or hiss, even in a silent room.

The piece that matters for dysautonomia is **who sets the gain**. Your auditory system is not a passive microphone. It is tuned moment to moment by attention, emotion, and the autonomic nervous system. The cochlea itself carries sympathetic nerve fibers that influence its blood supply, and the central hearing pathways sit right next to the limbic and autonomic circuits that run arousal. When the **sympathetic** "fight or flight" side is dominant, as it so often is in POTS, long COVID, and hyperadrenergic states, the whole system runs on high alert, and the auditory gain rides up with it.

This is why the same tinnitus can be nearly invisible one week and intrusive the next, with nothing changed about the ear. It is also why stress, a short night, caffeine, dehydration, or the start of a flare can all make it louder: each one nudges your nervous system further toward "fight or flight," and the gain follows. The flip side is the hopeful part. Calm the system down, and the volume usually comes down with it.

## The different ear symptoms, and what they mean

"My ears are weird" covers several very different experiences, and they point in different directions. Naming yours is the fastest way to know whether it is the ordinary autonomic kind you can track at home or one of the few that needs its own look.

| What you notice | What it feels like | Usually comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steady ringing, buzzing, or hissing | A constant tone or static, louder in quiet or on bad days | Central gain, set high by sympathetic arousal |
| Pulsatile whooshing | A rhythm that beats exactly in time with your heart | Hearing your own blood flow; needs a clinician to source |
| Ear fullness or pressure | Blocked, stuffy, or needing to pop your ears, hearing normal | Eustachian tube muscles run by autonomic nerves, allergy or mast cells |
| Sound sensitivity (hyperacusis) | Ordinary noise feels too loud or physically uncomfortable | The same amplified gain, plus a threat-primed nervous system |
| Fluctuating muffled hearing | Sound dips and returns, often with fullness | Eustachian or middle-ear pressure shifts |
| Ear or face pain with the noise | Aching around or in front of the ear, worse with jaw or neck | Jaw (TMJ) and neck structures feeding the hearing pathway |

The top row and the sound-sensitivity row are the **central gain** story: steady, worse under stress and in silence, and tightly linked to your autonomic state. The fullness and muffled-hearing rows are **pressure** stories from the Eustachian tube. The pulsatile row is its own category and the one to take seriously, which is covered below.

### Why total silence makes it worse

It feels backward, but a perfectly quiet room usually makes tinnitus louder, not quieter. With no sound to work with, the brain cranks its gain to the maximum, and the internal hum becomes the loudest thing in the room. This is why **sound enrichment** is a first-line comfort for almost everyone: a soft fan, quiet music, rain or nature sounds, or a white-noise track gives the system something real to listen to and lets the gain settle. The goal is a gentle background that sits just under the tinnitus, not loud masking.

### Why your ears feel full when your hearing is fine

The **Eustachian tube** equalizes the pressure behind your eardrum, and its tiny muscles are partly controlled by autonomic nerves. When that control is off balance, and especially when allergy or mast cell activity adds a little swelling, the tube opens and closes less smoothly. You feel this as fullness, popping, a blocked ear, or the urge to clear it, often with completely normal hearing on a test. Many people notice it swings with the weather, which is the same barometric sensitivity that shows up elsewhere in dysautonomia. If your ear pressure tracks the forecast, the piece on [barometric pressure and weather changes](/insights/pots/barometric-pressure-weather-changes-pots/) covers why.

### Why sound can feel like too much

Hyperacusis, where normal volumes land as uncomfortable or even painful, is the same amplification turned outward. The brain is boosting sound, so ordinary noise arrives louder than it should, and a nervous system biased toward threat detection is primed to react to it. It commonly overlaps with light sensitivity, migraine, and the general sensory overload that many people with POTS and long COVID describe. Pushing through loud environments tends to backfire; brief, gentle, graded exposure to everyday sound, with rest, usually serves the system better than either blasting it or hiding in silence.

## Pulsatile tinnitus: the one kind to take to a clinician

Most tinnitus is a steady tone the brain generates. **Pulsatile tinnitus** is different: it is a whooshing or thumping that beats in exact time with your heartbeat, because you are literally hearing blood move near your ear. You can usually confirm it by taking your pulse while you listen, the sound and the beat line up.

Pulsatile tinnitus becomes easier to hear whenever the heart is **fast and forceful**, which is the everyday state in POTS and hyperadrenergic dysautonomia, so a racing, pounding heartbeat on standing can genuinely make you hear your pulse. But it can also reflect things that have nothing to do with dysautonomia: anemia (a low blood count makes flow turbulent and loud), raised intracranial pressure, high blood pressure, or a blood-vessel variant near the ear. Raised intracranial pressure in particular overlaps with POTS and long COVID in some people, which is one more reason not to simply assume.

So the rule is simple. Steady, non-pulsatile ringing that rises and falls with your stress and sleep is the ordinary kind you can track. **True pulsatile tinnitus deserves a clinical evaluation** to work out the cause, especially if it is new, one-sided, or constant. It is usually not an emergency, but it is the one type you should not file under dysautonomia on your own.

## What makes the ordinary kind louder

Steady tinnitus and sound sensitivity are rarely random. They climb when several small things stack, and most of them are the same triggers that drive the rest of your autonomic load.

| Trigger | Why it turns the volume up |
| --- | --- |
| Short or broken sleep | Poor sleep raises sympathetic tone and lowers next-morning HRV, and tinnitus rides that gain |
| Stress and anxiety | Arousal primes the threat system, which directly amplifies and spotlights the sound |
| Dehydration and low salt | Lower blood volume means more cardiovascular strain and a hotter sympathetic state |
| Caffeine, nicotine, alcohol | Each shifts autonomic balance; effects are individual, so track yours |
| A flare or early illness | Rising sympathetic load and inflammation push the gain up before other symptoms peak |
| Total silence | No input means the brain cranks the gain to maximum |
| Jaw and neck tension | Somatic input from TMJ and the upper neck can feed and modulate the sound |

Two of those deserve a note. **Jaw and neck** involvement is common, especially for people with hypermobility, and it is often why the sound shifts when you clench, chew, or turn your head. If that is you, the overlap piece on [hypermobility, EDS, and POTS](/insights/pots/hypermobility-eds-and-pots/) is worth a read. And because the loudness is so tied to arousal, tinnitus frequently gets tangled up with anxiety, each one feeding the other. Untangling that loop is the same work as the rest of nervous system regulation, which is covered in [is it POTS or anxiety](/insights/pots/is-it-pots-or-anxiety/).

## Why tracking helps more than it sounds like it should

You cannot measure tinnitus with a sensor the way you can HRV or heart rate, because it is a perception. But you can rate it, and because the loudness follows your autonomic state so closely, a simple **0 to 10** rating logged each day turns out to be one of the more readable signals you have.

The move is to put that rating next to the things that set the gain: your [HRV](/insights/hrv/hrv-complete-guide/), your sleep, and your daily triggers. Over a few weeks a pattern almost always appears. Quiet-ear days line up with better sleep and higher HRV. Loud-ear days line up with short sleep, stress, dehydration, alcohol, or the first day of a flare. Sometimes the ears get loud a day before the rest of a crash arrives, which makes them a useful early-warning gauge rather than just a nuisance.

That does two things. It replaces helpless monitoring (listening to the sound, fearing it is getting worse forever) with a pattern you can act on. And it reassures, because watching the volume rise and fall with the rest of your recovery is proof that it is a **state**, not a one-way slide. The tracking playbook for this is the same as for any symptom, laid out in [find your triggers with a symptom journal](/insights/recovery/find-your-triggers-symptom-journal/).

### The levers that actually move it

Because the gain is set by your nervous system, the things that calm the system down are the things that quiet the ears:

- **Protect sleep.** It is the single biggest lever for next-day gain. Tinnitus and poor sleep feed each other, so breaking the loop in either direction helps.
- **Raise parasympathetic tone.** Slow breathing, in particular, shifts you toward "rest and digest" in minutes and is a reasonable thing to reach for when the sound spikes. The vagus nerve is the main route, covered in [how to stimulate the vagus nerve](/insights/hrv/how-to-stimulate-the-vagus-nerve/).
- **Use sound enrichment, not silence.** A soft background sound at bedtime and in quiet rooms gives the gain something to settle onto.
- **Pace and hydrate.** The same salt, fluids, and energy management that steady your orthostatic symptoms lower the sympathetic load the ears ride on.
- **Address the distress directly.** For intrusive tinnitus, evidence-based approaches like tinnitus-specific cognitive behavioral therapy and sound therapy target the alarm response, which is where the suffering mostly lives. Ask a clinician or audiologist.

None of this "cures" tinnitus, and anyone promising that is overselling. What it does is lower the volume and, just as important, lower the fear, which is often the louder of the two.

## When ear symptoms need a clinician, not a tracker

Most ear symptoms in dysautonomia and long COVID are the ordinary, trackable, volume kind. A few are not, and they are worth knowing cold:

- **Sudden hearing loss**, in one or both ears, over hours to a couple of days. This is a medical urgency. Sudden sensorineural hearing loss has a treatment window measured in days, so do not wait it out.
- **Tinnitus or hearing loss in only one ear** that persists. One-sided symptoms deserve an ENT evaluation to rule out causes that need their own workup.
- **True pulsatile tinnitus** that beats in time with your heart, as above, especially if new, one-sided, or constant.
- **Tinnitus with severe dizziness or vertigo, or with new neurological symptoms** like weakness, facial droop, or trouble speaking. Seek urgent care.
- **Ear pain with discharge or fever**, which points to infection rather than dysautonomia.

New, sudden, or one-sided ear symptoms always deserve a proper look rather than being assumed to be your nervous system. The point of understanding the ordinary kind is not to self-diagnose the rest away, it is to describe your symptoms well enough that you and your clinician can tell them apart.

## The bottom line

Ringing, buzzing, fullness, and sound sensitivity are common in POTS, dysautonomia, and long COVID, and most of the time they are a volume problem rather than a damage problem: the nervous system turns up the brain's own auditory gain, and you hear a background that was always there. That is why the sound gets louder on stressed, under-slept, low-HRV days and quieter on calm ones, and why the levers that help are the same ones that regulate the rest of your system. Rate it, track it next to your HRV and sleep, and it stops being a frightening mystery and becomes a signal you can read. Keep the short list of exceptions in mind, one-sided symptoms, sudden hearing loss, and true pulsatile tinnitus, and get those looked at properly.
