## The simplest tool for the shakiest moment

If you have POTS or orthostatic intolerance, you know the moment: you stand, and within a few seconds your heart races, your head goes light, and the room feels slightly unreliable. Most advice for that moment is about the long game, more salt, more fluids, more reconditioning, all of which matter. But there is also something you can do in the next five seconds, with no equipment and nobody noticing, that pushes back on the physiology directly. These are **counterpressure maneuvers**: brief, forceful muscle contractions that squeeze pooled blood back toward your heart and raise your blood pressure almost immediately.

They are not a cure, and they will not fix the underlying dysautonomia. What they do is buy you time and blunt the worst of a standing episode while the slower measures do their work. For reflex (vasovagal) fainting they have genuine randomized-trial support as a self-treatment, and the same muscle-pump physiology applies to the blood pooling that drives so many POTS symptoms. This is one of the few things on the list that is free, instant, and entirely in your control.

## Why counterpressure maneuvers work

To stand up is to fight gravity. The moment you are upright, roughly 300 to 800 ml of blood wants to settle into the veins of your legs and, especially, your abdomen (the splanchnic circulation). In a healthy system, reflexes clamp those vessels down and the blood keeps circulating. In POTS and orthostatic intolerance, that clamp-down is incomplete, so blood pools, less returns to the heart, and your body compensates with a sharp rise in heart rate. That is the standing spike. We go deeper into the pooling itself in [blood pooling in POTS legs](/insights/pots/blood-pooling-in-pots-legs/).

Your skeletal muscles are a second pump. The deep veins of your legs and abdomen run through and between large muscles, and when those muscles contract they squeeze the veins like a hand around a tube of toothpaste. One-way valves keep the blood moving in the right direction, toward the heart. Contract hard enough and you force a bolus of pooled blood back into central circulation, which refills the heart, raises stroke volume, and lifts blood pressure within seconds. Isometric contraction (tensing without really moving) also raises systemic vascular resistance, which adds to the pressure effect.

This is why the maneuvers that recruit the biggest muscles over the biggest venous reservoirs, the legs, buttocks and abdomen, are the most powerful. An arm squeeze helps, but it is working on a much smaller pool.

## The core maneuvers

There is no single correct maneuver. The right one is whichever you can actually do in the moment, which is why it helps to have two or three. Here are the ones with the best track record, roughly from most to least powerful for standing symptoms.

| Maneuver | How to do it | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leg crossing + tensing | Cross one leg tightly over the other while standing, then clench your thigh, buttock and abdominal muscles hard | Standing in line, at a counter, during conversation |
| Squatting | Lower into a squat or a deep knee bend; hold or rise slowly | A strong wave of lightheadedness when you can get low safely |
| Buttock + thigh clench | Feet apart, squeeze your glutes and thighs as if bracing; no visible movement | Discreet use when you cannot cross your legs |
| Abdominal tensing | Draw in and brace your core muscles firmly, like bracing for a push | Adds to any lower-body maneuver; targets the biggest reservoir |
| Handgrip | Grip an object or make tight fists and hold for 1 to 2 minutes | Seated situations, or as an early add-on |
| Arm tensing | Grip one hand with the other and pull your arms apart against the grip | When your legs are not available (driving passenger, wheelchair) |
| Bending / stooping | Bend forward at the waist, or put a foot up on a step and lean over your knee | Quick relief; raises pressure and lowers the head toward heart level |

A few notes that make them work better in practice:

- **Tense hard, not gently.** This is not a stretch. A real contraction, close to maximal effort, is what moves blood. Hold it for 20 to 30 seconds at a time, breathe (do not hold your breath, which can backfire), and repeat.
- **Stack them.** Crossing your legs *and* bracing your abdomen is better than either alone. The abdominal component is often the missing piece, since the splanchnic veins hold the largest share of the pooled blood, the same reason [abdominal compression beats socks](/insights/pots/compression-garments-for-pots/).
- **Lead with the legs when you can.** Lower-body maneuvers outperform handgrip and arm tensing for standing symptoms because of the muscle mass and venous volume involved.

## Timing is everything: use them early

The single biggest mistake is waiting. Counterpressure maneuvers are most effective as a *preemptive* or *very early* move, at the first warning sign rather than once you are already gray and tunnel-visioned.

For orthostatic symptoms, that means tensing your legs **before and as you stand**, not after you are swaying. For the warning signs of a faint, it means starting the moment you feel the classic pre-syncope cues: lightheadedness, a wave of warmth, nausea, sweating, ringing ears, or vision narrowing. Started early, the maneuvers can often abort or delay a reflex faint; started late, they are far less reliable. The landmark Physical Counterpressure Maneuvers Trial in people with recurrent vasovagal syncope found that teaching these moves roughly cut the burden of fainting episodes compared with conventional advice alone, and that benefit depended on using them at the first prodromal signs.

## How to tell if they are actually working

Counterpressure maneuvers *feel* like they help, and feelings are notoriously unreliable day to day with dysautonomia, where everything is already variable. The honest way to know is to measure.

The tool for this is the [orthostatic stand test](/insights/pots/the-orthostatic-stand-test-at-home/): lie down for a few minutes, record your resting heart rate, then stand and record your heart rate over the next several minutes. Do it one morning as a plain stand (no maneuver), and on another comparable morning use a lower-body maneuver as you stand and hold it. Compare the standing heart-rate rise and how quickly it settles. A smaller rise, a faster recovery, or fewer symptoms upright is real evidence that the maneuver is doing something for you.

A single pair of mornings is a start, but conditions vary (sleep, hydration, where you are in your cycle, the weather), so the trend over a couple of weeks is far more trustworthy than any one comparison. That is exactly the kind of before-and-after that is easy to lose track of in your head and easy to see on a chart.

## Building the habit

A maneuver only helps if it is automatic by the time you need it, so it is worth rehearsing when you are calm rather than inventing it mid-episode.

- **Pick two or three** that fit your life: one discreet (buttock and abdominal clench), one stronger (leg crossing), one for seated or legs-unavailable moments (handgrip or arm tensing).
- **Practice the "pre-stand" version** every time you get up from bed or a chair for a week: brace your legs and abdomen *before* you rise. It quickly becomes reflexive.
- **Pair them with your other tools.** Counterpressure works best stacked with the slower measures: adequate [salt and fluids](/insights/research/science-of-salt-and-fluids-for-pots/), [compression](/insights/pots/compression-garments-for-pots/), and gradual reconditioning. Think of them as the fast-acting layer on top, not a replacement.
- **Have a fallback.** If a maneuver is not holding and you are close to passing out, the safest action is to get low: sit or lie down and raise your legs. There is no prize for staying upright through a faint, and a fall is the real danger. More on that in [vasovagal syncope and fainting](/insights/basics/vasovagal-syncope-fainting/).

## When they are not the right answer

Counterpressure maneuvers are low-risk for most people, but a few cautions are worth stating plainly. Hard isometric contraction briefly raises blood pressure, so if you have uncontrolled hypertension, significant heart disease, or have been told to avoid straining, check with your clinician before relying on them. Do not hold your breath and bear down (a true Valsalva), which can drop your pressure when you release. And remember the ceiling: these moves manage symptoms in the moment. They do not treat the underlying POTS or dysautonomia, and a sudden change in your symptoms, new chest pain, or fainting without warning deserves medical evaluation rather than a workaround. For how the pieces of a full treatment plan fit together, see [POTS treatment: salt, fluids, compression and medication](/insights/pots/pots-treatment-salt-fluids-compression-medication/).

## The bottom line

Counterpressure maneuvers are the most immediate tool you have for the standing spike and the warning signs of a faint. By tensing your legs, buttocks and abdomen hard for a few seconds, you turn your skeletal muscles into a second pump that drives pooled blood back to your heart and raises your blood pressure within seconds. Leg crossing and squatting are the strongest, abdominal and buttock clenches are the most discreet, and handgrip covers the seated moments. Use them early, before symptoms peak, stack them with fluids and compression, and let your own stand test tell you whether they are working. They will not fix dysautonomia, but on a shaky day they can give you back the next minute, and sometimes that is exactly what you need.
