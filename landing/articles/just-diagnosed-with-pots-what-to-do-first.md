If you have just been told you have POTS, you probably feel two things at once: relief that it finally has a name, and confusion about what to do next. You may have waited a long time for this moment and then left the appointment with a handful of advice and a follow-up months away. **The first steps after a POTS diagnosis are mostly things you can start this week: more fluid and salt if your doctor agrees, compression, fewer triggers and a gentle exercise plan that starts lying down, plus a baseline so you can see whether they work.** Here is a plain first-weeks plan, built from what the guidelines actually say.

## What does a POTS diagnosis actually mean?

POTS (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome) means your heart rate rises too far when you stand, and you get symptoms such as lightheadedness, palpitations or shakiness that ease when you lie down. In adults the threshold is a sustained rise of at least 30 beats per minute within 10 minutes of standing, or 40 for people aged 12 to 19, without a big drop in blood pressure, and the symptoms have to have lasted at least 3 months ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024)).

A few things are worth knowing straight away. POTS is a syndrome, not one single disease, and several different processes can drive it, including low blood volume, nerve changes that let blood pool in the legs, an overactive stress response and deconditioning. Up to half of people can point to a trigger before it started, most often an infection, surgery or pregnancy ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024)). And you are far from alone: in the largest survey of people with POTS, 94% of 4,835 respondents were women, about half first had symptoms as teenagers, and the median wait for a diagnosis was 24 months ([Shaw et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.12895)).

The same guidelines say mortality is rarely linked to POTS and that many people improve with treatment. It is a real condition that can be hard to live with, but it is not a damaged heart. If you want the detail on how the diagnosis was made, see [how POTS is diagnosed](/insights/pots/how-pots-is-diagnosed/).

## What do guidelines say to try first?

Both the Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) and the Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) put non-drug steps first, either on their own or alongside medication if your symptoms are severe from the start ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024); [Sheldon et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hrthm.2015.03.029)). The HRS numbers below are as summarised in a later review ([Razavi and Safavi-Naeini, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7046364/)). The CCS also lists patient education as one of the main goals of treatment, so learning how your body responds is part of the plan, not a side project.

| Step | What the guidance says | Worth knowing |
|---|---|---|
| Fluid | CCS: at least 3 liters a day. HRS: 2 to 3 liters may be considered | Spread it through the day rather than in one go |
| Salt | CCS: 10 g of salt a day (about 4 g of sodium). HRS: 10 to 12 g | Agree the amount with your doctor first |
| Compression | CCS: waist-high, starting at 20 to 30 mm Hg | Compression below the thighs is unlikely to help |
| Exercise | CCS: at least 4 days a week, 30 minutes or more, starting non-upright | Can take 4 to 6 weeks to notice a change |
| Sleep | CCS: raise the head of the bed more than 10 degrees | Meant to reduce fluid loss overnight |

The salt advice deserves one warning. Ten grams of salt is far more than most health advice tells people to eat, and it is not safe for everyone, for example people with high blood pressure or kidney or heart disease. That is why it belongs on your list of questions for your doctor, not something to jump to on your own. The CCS suggests that if salt does not seem to be helping, a 24-hour urine sodium test can show whether you are actually getting enough. For the reasoning behind each of these, see [POTS treatment: salt, fluids, compression and medication](/insights/pots/pots-treatment-salt-fluids-compression-medication/).

<div class="callout callout-tip"><strong>Start one or two changes at a time.</strong> If you change fluid, salt, compression, sleep and exercise all in the same week, you will not know which one helped, and you will not know which one to blame if something makes you feel worse.</div>

## Which everyday triggers should you change now?

The CCS advises avoiding warm environments and prolonged standing, and teaching counter-pressure moves for when symptoms hit ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024)). Common everyday triggers to watch:

- **Heat:** hot showers, hot rooms, hot weather and saunas widen blood vessels and make pooling worse.
- **Standing still:** queues, cooking and showering standing up. Sit when you can, and shift your weight or cross your legs when you cannot.
- **Getting up quickly:** sit on the edge of the bed for a moment before standing, especially in the morning.
- **Big meals and alcohol:** both pull blood toward the gut or widen vessels. Smaller, more frequent meals are easier for many people.
- **Dehydration:** illness, heat and your period can all lower your fluid levels and make symptoms flare.

Counter-pressure moves are simple: crossing your legs and squeezing, tensing your thighs and buttocks, or squatting down when you feel faint. They push blood back toward your heart and can buy you time to sit or lie down safely. For what to do when a bad stretch hits anyway, see [POTS flare-ups: triggers and recovery](/insights/pots/pots-flare-up-triggers-and-recovery/).

## Should you start exercising?

For most people with POTS, yes. The CCS gives exercise training a strong recommendation, because it can ease symptoms, improve quality of life and in some people lead to remission ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024)). The key is where you start. Upright exercise is often too hard at first, so the CCS suggests beginning seated or lying down, with rowing machines, recumbent bikes or swimming, then slowly moving upright over weeks. It recommends sessions of at least 30 minutes, at least 4 days a week, and notes that it can take 4 to 6 weeks before some people notice improvement. That gap is where many people give up, so it helps to know in advance that week two is not the time to judge it.

There is one important exception. If you also have long COVID or ME/CFS and you get post-exertional malaise, a crash a day or two after effort, a standard POTS exercise plan can push you into repeated crashes. Talk to your doctor before starting, and read [POTS exercise or pacing: which advice applies to you](/insights/pots/pots-exercise-or-pacing-which-advice-applies/) to work out which camp you are in.

## What should you ask your doctor?

Your follow-up is easier if you go in with a short list. The CCS recommends that everyone being assessed for POTS gets a 12-lead ECG and routine blood tests: complete blood count, electrolytes, kidney function, ferritin, thyroid stimulating hormone and a morning cortisol, to look for other causes of a fast heart rate ([Raj et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2019.12.024)). It also recommends screening for linked conditions, because they can change how you respond to treatment and how things go over time.

Questions worth taking with you:

1. Is it safe for me to raise my salt and fluid, and by how much?
2. Could any medicine or supplement I already take be raising my heart rate? The CCS lists stimulants, including ADHD medications, among the examples. Do not stop anything on your own; ask.
3. Have my ferritin, thyroid and other basic blood tests been checked?
4. Should I be checked for any of the linked conditions above, such as hypermobility or migraine?
5. Is there a physiotherapist or exercise program you can refer me to?
6. If the first steps are not enough after a few weeks, what would we try next?

The CCS also says most people with POTS do not need tilt tables, specialist autonomic tests or brain scans, which are kept for people who do not respond to first-line treatment or have specific warning signs. If you are offered fewer tests than you expected, that is often why.

## How do you know if it's working?

Make a baseline before you change much, and then compare like with like. Three things are enough for most people:

- **A stand test:** lie down for at least 5 minutes, then stand for up to 10, noting your heart rate each minute, the same way clinicians measure it. Repeat it every week or two at the same time of day. See [the orthostatic stand test at home](/insights/pots/the-orthostatic-stand-test-at-home/).
- **A short daily symptom score:** lightheadedness, fatigue and brain fog on a simple 0 to 10 scale take seconds to log.
- **What you changed:** the day you started compression, the day you upped your salt. Without this, you will not remember what helped.

Then judge in weeks, not days. One good day after starting compression does not prove it works, and one bad day does not prove it failed. Compare a few weeks before and after each change, and bring the summary to your follow-up. [Turning your data into a doctor conversation](/insights/recovery/turn-your-data-into-a-doctor-conversation/) covers how to make that one page.

<div class="callout callout-note"><strong>A smaller rise counts.</strong> Many people still meet the POTS threshold even as they feel and function better. Look at your symptoms and what you can do, not only whether your heart rate rise drops below 30.</div>

## When is it more than POTS?

POTS can be frightening, but some symptoms point to something else and need prompt medical care rather than a wait for your follow-up. Get urgent help for chest pain with exertion or that does not settle when you lie down, fainting during exercise or while lying down, fainting with an injury, a racing heart that will not slow when you rest, or new weakness, numbness on one side, confusion or trouble speaking. The CCS keeps detailed heart tests for people with signs of structural heart disease or a rhythm problem not coming from the normal pacemaker of the heart, and brain tests for people with clear neurological signs, because those need a different workup.

## Will it get better?

For many people, yes, though usually slowly. In a Mayo Clinic follow-up, more than a third (37%) of adults no longer met the criteria for POTS a year later, and orthostatic symptoms improved in most ([Kimpinski et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mayocp.2012.02.020)). In 172 people whose POTS began in their teens, surveyed about five years after diagnosis, 19% reported complete resolution and about half more had improved but still had symptoms ([Bhatia et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpeds.2016.02.035)). Full recovery is not the most common outcome, but improvement is. More on that in [does POTS get better over time](/insights/pots/does-pots-get-better-over-time/).

## The bottom line

A POTS diagnosis is a starting point, not a verdict. The guidelines start with things you can begin this week: more fluid and salt if your doctor agrees, waist-high compression, fewer triggers like heat and long standing, and an exercise plan that starts lying down or seated. Ask for basic blood work including ferritin, a review of your current medicines and screening for linked conditions. Record a baseline before you change much, add one change at a time and give each a few weeks before judging it.

Talk to your doctor before changing your salt or fluid intake, activity, treatment or medication. If your symptoms are new, worsening or frightening, get medical care.</div>
