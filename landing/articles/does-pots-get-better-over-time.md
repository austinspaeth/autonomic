If you are early in POTS, or deep into a bad stretch of it, the question underneath all the others is simple and heavy: is this forever? You want a straight answer, so here is one. **For most people POTS does get better over time, but it usually improves gradually rather than disappearing, and a meaningful share keep some symptoms for years.** Follow-up studies consistently show that most patients improve, that younger people and those whose [POTS](/insights/pots/pots-subtypes-explained/) followed a virus tend to do best, and that full, lasting disappearance of symptoms is real but not the most common ending. The rest of this walks through what the research actually found, who recovers fastest, and how to see your own direction of travel instead of judging it from one exhausting week.

These are educational field notes, not medical advice. Nothing here diagnoses or treats anything, and your own course depends on your subtype, your triggers and your care, which is a conversation for your clinician.

## Most people improve, but few fully recover

It helps to separate three different things that get blended into one word, "better." There is *symptom improvement* (you feel and function better than you did), *no longer meeting the diagnostic threshold* (your heart rate no longer jumps the required amount on standing), and *complete resolution* (symptoms gone). These do not move together, and most of the hope in POTS lives in the first two rather than the third.

POTS is defined as a sustained rise in heart rate of at least 30 beats per minute within 10 minutes of standing (40 beats for people under 19), with chronic symptoms and without a drop in blood pressure that would make it [orthostatic hypotension](/insights/pots/orthostatic-hypotension-vs-pots/) instead ([Vernino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.autneu.2021.102828)). Across the follow-up studies below, a large share of people stop meeting that strict heart-rate threshold over time and most report feeling better, even when some symptoms linger. So "does POTS get better?" and "does POTS go away completely?" have genuinely different answers, and it is worth holding both.

## What the follow-up studies actually found

Three studies, with very different follow-up lengths, give the clearest picture. They measured slightly different things and followed different groups, so they are placed side by side here for scale, not combined into one number.

<div class="table-wrap">

| Study | Who, and follow-up | What they found |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kimpinski et al., 2012 | 58 adults, 1 year | 37% no longer met tilt-table criteria for POTS; orthostatic symptoms improved in most; "a relatively favorable prognosis in most patients" |
| Bhatia et al., 2016 | 172 people, POTS began in their teens, ~5 years later | 19% fully resolved, 51% improved but with persistent symptoms, 16% intermittent symptoms; about 86% improved overall |
| Bourne et al., 2026 | 44 adults, median 23 years from onset | 2% fully resolved, 46% improved, 11% unchanged, 25% worsened, 16% variable; all but one still had some symptoms |

</div>

Read together, these tell a consistent story. Within the first year, a large minority of adults already improve enough to fall below the POTS threshold ([Kimpinski et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22795533/)). Over several years, the great majority improve, and roughly one in five who developed POTS as teenagers recover completely ([Bhatia et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26979650/)). And over decades, while nearly half still describe themselves as improved, most adults keep at least some symptoms, and a sizable group do not improve or get worse ([Bourne et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.70104)). Improvement is the most common direction. Total disappearance is the exception, not the rule.

## Does age of onset change the odds?

Yes, and it is one of the clearest signals in the research: younger age at onset points to a better outlook. POTS most often begins in the teen years, and that group tends to do well. In the 5-year follow-up above, about 86% of people whose POTS started in adolescence had improved, and nearly one in five had fully resolved ([Bhatia et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26979650/)). Dysautonomia International describes the typical pattern plainly: most people who develop POTS during puberty or adolescence usually recover by their mid-teens to early twenties ([Dysautonomia International, 2024](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/conditions.php?ID=1)).

That hopeful picture is real, but two cautions keep it honest. First, "improved" did most of the lifting in that study, not "cured," and even the recovered teens often still carried some physical limitation years later. Second, adult-onset POTS can also improve, just with a lower chance of full resolution, as the 23-year adult survey showed. If you want to understand the adolescent picture in depth, see our guide to [POTS in teenagers](/insights/pots/pots-in-teenagers-and-adolescents/).

## Why post-viral and long COVID POTS may differ

How your POTS started seems to matter. When POTS follows a clear viral illness, the outlook is often better than average. Estimates suggest that about 50% of people with post-viral POTS recover fully or almost fully within a two to five year window ([Dysautonomia International, 2024](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/conditions.php?ID=1)). A viral trigger is common in these studies; in the 1-year adult follow-up, about a third of patients reported a viral infection before their symptoms began ([Kimpinski et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22795533/)).

Long COVID has made post-viral POTS far more common, and it is also the newest version of the question, which means the long-term data is still being gathered. Early follow-up of people with [long COVID and POTS](/insights/postviral/is-it-long-covid-or-pots/) suggests many improve over the first year or two, but a share continue to have symptoms past that, and researchers are explicit that tracking these patients over longer periods is still needed. The practical message is the same whether your trigger was COVID, another virus or none that you can name: the base rate favors improvement, especially in the first few years, and your own measured trend will tell you more than any average.

## What makes a better or worse outlook

No single factor decides your course, but the research points to patterns worth knowing, so you can read your own situation with clearer eyes rather than fear.

| Tends to point toward improvement | Tends to point toward a longer course |
| --- | --- |
| Onset in the teen years or young adulthood | Onset later in adulthood |
| A clear viral trigger before symptoms began | No identifiable trigger, or a connective-tissue or autoimmune driver |
| Early diagnosis and consistent treatment | Long delay to diagnosis and untreated years |
| Graded, paced activity and avoiding deconditioning | Prolonged bed rest and ongoing deconditioning |
| The first few years after onset (most change happens early) | A plateau many years in |

None of these are destiny, and plenty of people improve against the odds. But they explain why two people with the same diagnosis can have very different stories, and why so much of standard POTS care, from [pacing](/insights/recovery/pacing-101-energy-envelope/) to graded activity, is really about nudging your odds in the right direction over time.

## Is POTS dangerous or permanent?

Two reassurances are worth stating plainly, because the fear attached to this question is often bigger than the facts. First, POTS is not a progressive disease in the way that word usually frightens people. It is not known to damage the heart or other organs or to shorten life expectancy, and there is currently no evidence that it marches steadily worse over the years for most patients. Second, "chronic" is not the same as "permanent." POTS is chronic, meaning it tends to last a while, and there is no cure, yet a good share of patients recover and most improve with treatment and time ([Dysautonomia International, 2024](https://dysautonomiainternational.org/conditions.php?ID=1)).

The honest counterweight is that for some people POTS is genuinely long-lasting and disruptive. In the 23-year adult survey, roughly a third were unable to work because of POTS, and employment had dropped over the follow-up ([Bourne et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.70104)), echoing the roughly one in four of patients that Dysautonomia International describes as disabled and unable to work. Improvement being common does not make the hard cases rare or imaginary. Both things are true, and holding both is more useful than either false comfort or despair.

## How to tell if you are actually getting better

Here is the real trap. POTS improves over months and years, but it varies enormously from day to day, so on any given morning you cannot feel the trend underneath the noise. A good week after a bad one feels like luck; a bad week after a good one feels like relapse. Memory is a terrible instrument for slow change, which is exactly why so many people who are improving still feel stuck.

The fix is to measure a few stable things the same way and watch the line over time, not the dots.

| What to track | Why it reflects your trend |
| --- | --- |
| Morning HRV and resting heart rate | Shift slowly as your autonomic balance improves; less noisy than how you feel |
| A periodic [stand test](/insights/pots/the-orthostatic-stand-test-at-home/) | Your heart-rate rise on standing is the core POTS measure; a smaller rise over months is real progress |
| Symptom load and good days per week | The lived outcome, averaged across weeks so one bad day does not dominate |
| Activity you can handle without a crash | A rising ceiling is one of the earliest signs of recovery |

Compare months, not mornings. See [what recovery actually looks like](/insights/recovery/what-recovery-looks-like/) for the shape of a real, non-linear climb, and [how to read recovery from post-viral dysautonomia](/insights/recovery/recovery-from-post-viral-dysautonomia/) for turning scattered data into decisions. Do this for a season or two and the question stops being a guess.

## The bottom line

Does POTS get better over time? For most people, yes, but slowly, unevenly, and more often as meaningful improvement than as complete disappearance. Within a year, a large minority of adults already stop meeting the strict POTS criteria. Over several years, the majority improve and about one in five teen-onset cases fully recover, with post-viral cases doing especially well, roughly half recovering within two to five years. Over decades, nearly half of adults still describe themselves as improved, though most keep some symptoms and a minority do worse. POTS is not progressive or life-shortening, there is no cure yet, and the single most useful thing you can do is stop judging your course by one exhausting week and start watching your own trend across months. The direction is usually upward. You just have to zoom out far enough to see it.
